“My Random Thoughts Notebook43 Is My Hobby”

Animage, December 1995

Respectable Japanese do not involve themselves in war and military affairs. But I’ve been interested in these topics since childhood, a fact that I dare not declare in public. If I declared this in public, I would be considered pro-war.

Politically, I was against rearming Japan, and even now I oppose sending peacekeeping troops abroad. Despite this I have been curious about military affairs all along. We can say that there are many strange things in this world, things that make me wonder about the foolishness that has been done. Absorbing trivia for decades does mount up; and when it mounts up I want to divulge it, so I draw these fantasies … [laughs]

Since these are fantasies, I insist that there is no value in them as reference material. It is too much trouble to research history just to draw airplanes and battleships exactly as they were. My mind would go dizzy doing that, so I am drawing them only as an approximation of reality. I mix in a lot of false information as well. If those who are very familiar with history are taken in, I am extremely happy. [laughs]

This serialization is the ultimate in irregularity. When I finish a film, the editors at Model Graphics call me up and say, “Shall we begin soon?” I respond, “Can you wait a little longer?” [laughs]

Actually, I was able to draw best when I was serializing the Nausicaä manga. Drawing those kinds of pictures, they add up. After staying up till dawn drawing the last manga pages to meet the printer’s deadline, I would draw these models the next day; each would take about a week. While drawing Nausicaä my mind kept straying to what kinds of models I would draw. I wanted to draw this and that. When I finished the drawings, I would start on my next manga series. Under these conditions I could keep up my pace. But it is impossible to keep it up while making movies.

After all, it is hard work to bring a film inside my head to life. Each drawing is just one frame, and within that one frame is a lot of fantasy. Sometimes I begin to think that these drawings could become a sequence. There are several stories that I would like to turn into films if the opportunity arose. But when I think it over I wonder whether the story is deserving of so much labor, whether there is enough there. It seems to me that even when the finances are in place, there are some projects I just shouldn’t do. In essence, it is my hobby to draw seemingly real vehicles. It works as my psychological release valve.

For this radio drama I am not facing any challenges. [laughs] After all, this is just my hobby. Someone told me he wanted to make a radio drama out of the drawings I had done as a hobby, and I said go ahead and do as you like. I don’t feel like I am making a big effort. [laughs]

I think radio dramas deserve more acknowledgement than they get. To me they are the best. When I am reading books and considering whether they would make good films, I find many that would make great radio dramas.

But the images and imagination stimulated by the radio dramas turn flat when projected as film images. The images are restricted to the specific pictures one draws. But if we say “a woman of celestial beauty” in a radio drama, she will be “a woman of celestial beauty” for each of us! After all, we are calling her a woman of celestial beauty. Hearing this, the listener imagines a combination of all the women of celestial beauty that he can think of to create his own “woman of celestial beauty.” This is quite breathtaking. For this reason, too, it would be a shame if radio dramas were to disappear from the air.

I love the blank space that radio dramas provide. In a case like Rampo Edogawa,44 his world becomes very banal when it is depicted in film. Just think, his detective Kogorō Akechi seems an idiot, as does the Boy Detective Club. They drop a badge at the side of the road. I wonder what would happen if an unintended person picks it up. Of course, that’s neither here nor there. [laughs]

Rampo Edogawa’s brilliance is in the psychological darkness he portrays. It is not a physical darkness in actual space. He has captured the psychological darkness that lies within. For example, The Strange Tale of Panorama Island (Panoramatō kitan) becomes tacky when made into a film, as it comes off as a cheap theme park ride. But when the story is told in an evocative manner, something very deep comes through. This depth can only be revealed in a radio drama. When you listen to it alone … As a child I felt chills hearing the voice on the radio talk about the rasping sound of the clothing worn by a woman of celestial beauty.

But they didn’t provide that sound effect. The narrator Musei Tokugawa said “the rasping sound of her clothing.” [laughs] What is the rasping sound of clothing? As a boy, I would wonder and feel shivers of anticipation. And now, when I am working late with the radio on and come across a radio drama, I feel lucky. I think these dramas are fun to listen to. But a while ago they became overly artistic. Those were uninteresting. Unless there’s some narration, I don’t think radio dramas are interesting.

“Random Thoughts Notebook” might also be sufficient with a single narrator. After all, it’s not as though I’d drawn different characters. And just because various people are lined up at the microphone doesn’t mean that the expression is more fully realized. I think that when one person narrates, the stories tend to find a fuller expression. This is especially true with the kind of material I created. They did tell me, “Do as you please.” And this was done as a hobby … I realize I have talked a lot, especially about my hobby. Ha ha ha ha!

41 Takei Sanseidō is a stationery store established at the beginning of the Meiji period (1868–1912). In 1927, Ryūkichi Takei (second generation) had a shop built at 1-Chome, Kandasudachō, Chiyoda-ku, Tokyo. In the beginning it mainly sold calligraphy items, such as brushes, sumi-ink, and inkstones, but later it sold a full line of items including paint brushes and stationery goods. It was eventually dismantled and rebuilt at the Edo-Tokyo Open Air Architectural Museum in Koganei, Tokyo, where it remains today.

42 A small bath with steep sides, traditionally made of wood. They are generally used for warmth and relaxation, not bathing proper.

43 This interview was given when “Miyazaki Hayao no zassō nōto” (Hayao Miyazaki’s Random Thoughts Notebook [irregularly serialized in Model Graphics, 1984–1990; published as one volume by Dai Nippon Kaiga in 1992]) was set to be broadcast as a radio drama by Nippon Broadcasting System (Nippon Hōsō).

44 Rampo Edogawa (October 21, 1894–July 28, 1965); Japanese author of detective fiction. His pen name is a Nipponization of Edgar Allan Poe.
