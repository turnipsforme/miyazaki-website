—I’d first like to ask why you decided to create a new studio. Some very high quality films have already been made in your current studio in Kichijōji, including Castle in the Sky, My Neighbor Totoro, Grave of the Fireflies, and Kiki’s Delivery Service. Why can’t you just keep using it?

Miyazaki: Well, first of all, it was just too small. It was so small that we had to split the staff up just to work there—so small that, as you know, in Kichijōji we now have Studio 1 and Studio 2. With all the people we also have outside our studio, we’re looking at a total of nearly a hundred staff members. So I felt that we needed a place where everyone could get together in one place, where we could communicate better. But no matter how hard we searched, we just couldn’t find the right building. If such a building existed, we would have rented it. So that, in a nutshell, is why we are creating a new studio. There are quite a few general purpose, shared buildings in the area, but even if these are designed to be offices it still seems to me that they’re mainly designed to look good when someone from the outside comes to visit; they’re not really designed to make the jobs of the people working there any easier. They have too many problems—the ladies’ restrooms are too narrow, or there are no rooms to rest in if people feel ill—so we were forced to conclude that we were better off building a brand new studio.

—Is there really a big advantage in having all the staff in one place?

Miyazaki: I think there is a big advantage. First of all, different sections of the organization can easily gather together in one place and discuss matters amongst themselves.

But I do have to say, there’s no guarantee that we’re going to create great works just because we’ve created a new studio. Creating a building doesn’t guarantee we’re going to be able to foster good employees. After all, there are lots of examples in the past of wonderful works being created in truly horrible environments. I’m not so deluded that I think we’re going to create great works just because we’ve built a nice studio. And I’m not particularly interested in becoming the owner-manager of a studio, for that matter, either. But it is true that the environment thus far—for those of us who have been working here—has been unacceptable. I don’t know if the new place will help foster great new talent, but we wanted to at least give the next generation of animators a good work space.

—I imagine it’s going to cost a huge amount of money, given the escalation in property values and construction fees.

Miyazaki: Yes, that’s what they say. And most of it will be paid with loans. Some people might wonder whether we’ll be able to continue working with big debts, but with or without debts, if we can’t keep working, we can’t keep working. Animation is a business, and it seems to me that it’s probably best to make the investment and take the risk. Some studios try to create works on an almost guerilla basis, but then the problem is that the staff may not stick around. If we’re going to moan and groan about too many listless animated works being created recently, when the people with drive and motivation aren’t assuming any risk at all, then we’ve got a problem too. So that’s why I think there is real meaning in improving the working environment.

Highlights of the new studio

—Were you the one who came up with the original plan for a new studio?

Miyazaki: Yes. Others wanted to work on this, too, but I happened to have the time then, so I’m the one who drew up the plans.

—When you designed the studio, were there any other studios that you used as a reference?

Miyazaki: No, not particularly. But in designing it, I did try to take into account what I imagined would be easier and more convenient for me when directing. For example, I tried to make it super easy for me to get from my desk to a conference, or to a discussion with the various production sections, or to the clean-up department. And then, when people from the clean-up department had to go to another job, I also tried to consider things like what course they would take or when someone from the art department went to see a screening, how they would walk there, etc. …

—What are some other highlights of the new studio?

Miyazaki: Well, we actually planned a room to take a break in, which we call “the bar.” I hate to see people forced to eat lunch at their desks, so we designed it so several people could get together and eat their boxed bento lunches or drink tea. Until now the in-between artists, the art department people, and the clean-up people have been too scattered about to get together easily, so I wanted to create a place where they could gather and communicate with each other if need be, and even have parties. We therefore located the bar in the center of the studio so that it would be easy for people from any section to get to, and right in front of the bar we created a spiral staircase. And we will also plant as many shrubs and trees as possible around the building. This won’t be done just for the employees, but also because we don’t want to ruin the surrounding environment. To discourage employees who commute from leaving their bicycles or motorbikes in the neighborhood, we’ll have an extra-large parking lot with a roof. Hopefully, the neighborhood will be improved a bit by our building this new studio.

A base to create films

—I’m sure there are many other highlights of the new studio, but let me ask, in conclusion, what was the basic concept you used in designing it?

Miyazaki: The main idea was to build a base to create animated movies in Japan; we would, of course, have to depend on the help of outside contractors, but we wanted a studio that was not completely dependent on them, that would also have in-house employees whose talents could be fostered as we worked. We knew that we couldn’t accomplish this overnight, but we wanted to at least start with enough space to do the actual filming.

In other words, I thought it would be too hard to maintain our direction if we kept expanding while maintaining only a few production staff in a few rooms and farming everything out or sending everything overseas. I think this came out of the same ideas used when soliciting new employees.

—When do you expect the studio to be finished?

Miyazaki: We haven’t started construction yet, but if all goes according to plan, we hope to be able to move into the new facility around July of next year.
