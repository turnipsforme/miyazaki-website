▶ on the peripheryof the work

At the University of Tokyo’s Science Fiction Club, I saw a screening of Mister Bug Goes to Town and three works from the Color Classics series—The Song of the Birds, Somewhere in Dreamland, and Small Fry. I returned home feeling very satisfied, but the more I thought about it later, the more I was overcome with disappointment and frustration.

What I am about to say here may already have been pointed out by others and thus be superfluous, but it represents my true feelings, so I hope readers will indulge me.

I like Fleischer works. And when I say “Fleischer,” I do not mean Dave Fleischer the individual, but the whole animation staff, which probably included members both young and old and represented an entire trend in animation that is symbolized by the word “Fleischer.” In fact, I had a strong sense that Mister Bug Goes to Town was a work that might not have even been created or animated by Dave Fleischer. This was the first of the problems that I had.

I also thought that all three of the Color Classics works I saw that day were complete flops. They appeared to have been created by some tasteless artist trying too hard to impress, but who ultimately hadn’t tried hard enough. The themes hadn’t been developed sufficiently, the drawings weren’t clean, and the use of three-dimensional miniatures damaged the sense of space in the films and—instead of creating a new world for us—wound up looking fake and cheap. And it was overly obvious that being unable to insert the miniatures between the animation cels and the camera was a fatal flaw. Was the fact that the staff tripped up in the use of miniatures a result of Fleischer making too many demands regarding the depiction of space? I don’t think so. I think it was the result of someone having had an inadequate understanding of space. And, at the same time, I think it was the result of someone not having had enough faith in the power of drawings.

In both Popeye and Mr. Bug we begin to perceive the space on the screen after we’ve given up on the miniatures. Then, whether it’s the sharpness of the spatial sense occasionally seen in Popeye, or the superior spatial composition in Mr. Bug, there is a quality not seen in other Fleischer works. While mere speculation on my part, I suspect that a few of the staff on these two productions later developed into truly talented animators. The Fleischers pursued an almost crude and vulgar vision, but the chaos resulting from their vision was also filled with power, and it gave birth to some highly talented staff members with a more refined power, in terms of both technique and sensitivity. These members, it seems to me, must have become central to subsequent Fleischer productions.

Having first honed their talents on Gulliver’s Travels, in Mr. Bug I think these individuals were able to surpass Fleischer. Nowhere in the Color Classics do we see anything like the best ideas in Mr. Bug, or the skills demonstrated by the way the ideas are assembled into a story and the screen is composed.

My opinions are of course highly personal and biased, but they are also impressions formed by my professional instincts. The decadence in Betty Boop leads right into the squalid nature of the Color Classics. Rather than say that I like the over-the-top and unsettling nature of Betty Boop, I should say that I’m amazed by it. And what I sense in Betty Boop is probably the same basic energy that was used to create Popeye. But what starts with Gulliver and is perfected with Mr. Bug is simply too strange and too different from what we see in those other works. I’d even like to suggest that this shift may have reflected a generational turnover in the production staff. When talented people start emerging in the animation industry, the changes in production leadership are not always reflected in the credits of a film, especially over the span of only a year or so. I’m just speculating here, of course, but I even wonder if a clear split might not have appeared between Fleischer animators who were content to continue business as usual and those who wanted to explore new directions. I can even imagine that there would have been a few arguments.

Despite this, Mr. Bug is way too Fleischer-like. Relative to the level of technical perfection, the theme isn’t fully developed and as usual it shows. Perhaps this is one of the great weaknesses of works directed by Dave Fleischer. That, at least, is my frustration as a viewer. The bugs climb up a building and their earnestness is heartwarming. I love the composition as a fully animated scene, but doesn’t the film’s ending leave us feeling awfully empty?

What I found so frustrating is the emptiness or meaninglessness of the “nature” depicted on the roof of the skyscraper; rather than the sort of nature we refer to today, the film seems to really show a wealthy private individual, living protected by the power of money. Fleischer, the director, chooses very up-to-date elements and deals with profoundly modern themes, but here he seems to be secretly swapping them for his own ambition to succeed as an artist. What was really too much for me, and made me consciously look away in disgust, is that he creates a sun room in a rooftop garden—a garden probably outfitted with artificial turf that would be a hard place for insects to live!—where he shows a composer and his wife penning popular songs.

The line at the very end of the film—about the humans on the streets way below looking like bugs—seems reasonable at first, but it’s no more profound than anything a small child might say when standing on the observation deck on the fifty-second floor of the triangular Sumitomo Bank building in Tokyo and looking down at the ground. To me, the director merely seems to be showing us some bugs looking down at their fellow bugs and feeling smug because they’ve transcended being bugs.

To create a good cartoon movie animators have to lie and audiences expect us to lie well. But some lies are over the top, and the ending in Mr. Bug Goes to Town definitely falls into that category. I’m sure the staff got all excited when they came up with the idea of having the bugs escape from their situation by climbing up a skyscraper under construction. But in reality that wouldn’t work. If you ask me, they probably never would have made it to the top of the building; instead, they would have been forced to seek out new territory somewhere outside of the city for a place to live. They’d have become like Chaplin in Modern Times, or been forced to flee like the families in The Grapes of Wrath.

I know I shouldn’t criticize others, but why do the final scenes of cartoon movies always have to be so ridiculous? This was true with Snedronningen (The Snow Queen); its ending was that film’s greatest flaw. And the ending of La Bergère et le Ramoneur (Adventures of Mr. Wonderful) makes it look like the production staff went out to have a wrap party. Not only that, at the ending of Hakujaden (The Tale of the White Serpent), Bai-Niang looks truly stupid. In fact, when I saw Mushi Productions’ Aru machikado no monogatari (Tales of a Street Corner), it was the first time I was really able to say “sayonara” to Osamu Tezuka. The story is all dressed up with the theme of war. It’s a case of narcissism revolving around an apocalyptic beauty (and I really hate to use the word “beauty” here). As was true with Space Battleship Yamato, there’s a uniquely repugnant aspect to Tezuka’s generation, and it manifests in this film where a man and woman shown in posters on the street go up in flames. In spite of their burning up, the little girl character survives. It’s almost as if Tezuka avoided depicting a nuclear war and showed a conventional war instead, just so the girl could survive. Rather than being modern, the fascism and the bombs he depicted in the film wound up looking more like something from a historical drama.

To depict a war head-on in animation is to choose to depict a subject just as preposterous as the life of bugs in a city. But even if you’re only making a cartoon film, the moment you choose such a subject you should not flinch from pursuing it. In the case of Tales of a Street Corner, I think the only correct approach would have been to show that the little girl’s death was completely meaningless!

Both those who make cartoon films and those who love them tend to have a certain immaturity to them, and they tend to go easy on each other. But I’d like to see effort put into filmmaking sufficient to withstand the bare-knuckled criticism that I’m providing here. Cartoons have certain weaknesses that we normally don’t notice, and a type of laxity arises precisely because the films are treated as mere cartoons. When discussing films, I therefore believe we should expose both problems—the weaknesses of animation and the laxity in discussing them—to the full light of day. Most importantly, even if a film develops an extraordinarily stupid theme, we have a responsibility to find other themes that conversely show more brilliance. Several of the Popeye films are absolutely first-rate, whereas Mr. Bug Goes to Town is only second-rate.

My new conclusion is therefore as follows: Mr. Bug Goes to Town is both wonderful and incredibly stupid. People say Dave Fleischer created it, but I would like to extend my heartfelt greetings and congratulations to the nameless staff members who managed to claw their way out of his control. (It’s okay if they only exist in my imagination.) I do wonder where they went. They probably scattered throughout the industry, lost their powers, and either went through a masturbatory period of creating Fleischer’s Superman, or disappeared into doing work on not particularly memorable films.

—May 7, 1979
