Interviewer: Hiroaki Ikeda, Roman Album My Neighbor Totoro, June 3, 1988

Scene setting and the house

—Where did you have in mind as the setting for My Neighbor Totoro, Miyazaki-san?

Miyazaki: The setting for the story is actually taken from many places. It is a combination of the area near Seijō Sakuragaoka where Nippon Animation is located, the area surrounding the Kandagawa River where I grew up, and the landscape of Tokorozawa where I live now. And the art director Kazuo Oga-san is from Akita in the north, so it looks a bit like Akita as well. [laughs] So I didn’t have a specific location in mind.

—When did the story take place? I had heard it was to be around 1957–58, but watching it I wondered if images from your childhood were included.

Miyazaki: It’s actually a lie to say it was set in the latter 1950s; the truth is that it is a story set in a time before television. At first I thought of having the radio broadcast Shinshokoku monogatari57 in the story, but that seemed too blatant. I purposely got rid of anything like that. I thought that the earlier time frame would be handled by Grave of the Fireflies, which was on the same bill.

—So the only hint of the time is the Shigeru Sugiura manga scribbled in Kanta’s notebook?

Miyazaki: That was put in by the original artist, Masako Shinohara, as her personal preference. Anyone who recognizes that drawing is pretty old by now. [laughs]

—Come to think of it, our grade-school notebooks always had blank areas around each page like that, just asking for doodles.

Miyazaki: My notebooks were full of doodles. So I only used a bit of the pages for my classes. [laughs] I drew all over the back of test papers … We all have those memories. Rather than going abroad to hunt for locations, I was really happy to make a movie full of fragments of landscapes I had seen in Japan, or remembered, or had seen in my childhood. For example, the large camphor tree in Tsukamori, the forest—although there wasn’t really such a large tree, I felt I had seen such a large tree. We all have some tree we looked up at in awe of its size and grandeur when we were little. When I drew the tree it turned out to be that huge. I don’t feel at all that I am lying because in my mind it really was that big.

—In the initial scene when they are moving in, Satsuki and Mei run around the house. I remembered my childhood, thinking, “That’s true, a house with no furniture is big.” Going to a seaside house and opening up unfamiliar rooms in a race with my brothers was always a lot of fun. The reason the house seemed strangely large was because there was no furniture.

Miyazaki: We’ve all had that kind of experience once or twice. I like that scene as well even though it isn’t an important one. We would run around the house, opening a door, saying “It’s not here!” then closing the door, opening another and saying “The toilet!” As we ran around, we got excited and started laughing—that’s really the child’s world. But when your kids do that in front of you, it’s awfully noisy. I didn’t have to use any director’s tricks in this film. In fact, I made it wondering if it really was all right to use so few tricks. It was easy to direct this work.

—That house they moved into, was that so that when their mother was released from the hospital she would have a place to live where there was fresh, clean air?

Miyazaki: Yes, that’s right. There’s no other reason. It was quite normal to see a Japanese-style house with a Western-style attached room in the old days. Actually, that house was only half-completed. The garden, too—before a nice garden could be landscaped, the house was no longer needed … In other words, it’s a house where a sick person has died.

—Is that the backstory?

Miyazaki: My thought is that basically that house was a villa that had been built with an attached room in which a sick person could recuperate. The attached room was for a tuberculosis patient. Since that person has died, the house is no longer needed and is empty. That’s the backstory.

So the old woman was probably a maid hired to work in the house. That’s why she is direct in the way she speaks, unlike most country people. As this is the backstory there’s no need to explain all this, so I didn’t tell anyone. But in my mind that’s how I thought of the house. The fact that the attached room is so sunny also speaks to this setting.

A world we can recall having seen

—Watching the film, I was very taken with the intermediate colors in the backgrounds.

Miyazaki: That owes a lot to Oga-san’s artwork. It is his artwork that allows such a thorough expression of the flavor of that world. I suspect he himself didn’t think he could do that much. For myself, other than the totoro, the cat bus, and the soot sprites, I used things I had actually seen. They weren’t things I had read about in a book, but they were all things and scenes I recall seeing—the house, the land, the water’s surface, the trees, and the plants. In that respect making the film was a delightful process. If the setting were a foreign country, I wouldn’t know about those things: what would be there when a door is opened or what kind of flowers bloom on the roadside.

The actual setting isn’t nearby either. In effect, for Oga-san and myself, it was much better to depict a real scene than to show a make-believe setting as if it were real. That was what I wanted to come close to doing. I wanted to put into the images the feeling I’ve had on looking at a grassy field and thinking, “There are so many kinds of plants growing here.” We had some difficulties capturing this mood, but I was happy with the approach we took to the background art. Oga-san and I had many discussions about the story being set in summer, and during the solstice the sun wouldn’t be this high, or the sun would be higher so that the sun’s rays wouldn’t go that far into the veranda, and what we should do about it.

—I saw some of the cels and realized that the grassy field wasn’t just background art, it was also book-processed58 in front, and several shots of grass were added—they were treated in various ways.

Miyazaki: That’s because when cut at the joint line, the outlines of the trees turn out badly. I’ve been doing that for a while. For grassy fields and such I don’t want to draw it entirely using a harmony59 technique, so I add a bit of harmony leaves to the cels I put on top of the backgrounds, just enough so it can’t be detected. I guess you could tell because there was too much grass. I tried to use a more classical method this time around. I intentionally limited the use of backlighting, for instance.

—The camera angle was always at eye level, it seems.

Miyazaki: That was the way it ended up in order to give the most natural feel. It wouldn’t fit the story to use a remote pan or set the camera low at ground level.

—The bus stop scene had one different shot.

Miyazaki: Yes, that was a high angle shot.

—I wondered if it was a prelude to Totoro’s sudden appearance. [laughs]

Miyazaki: Well, actually, we used it because it was too much to shoot the long scene from only one angle. The rain worked really well. If we can go that far, then we get more and more greedy and next want to draw the rain as it splashes up from hitting the ground. We were able to give a much more natural look than we initially thought. Someone who saw the film said, “Japan’s nature is so beautiful,” and I thought, “We did it!” It’s the kind of landscape we see all the time. This kind of landscape still exists in many places.

—Today, too. Since it snowed yesterday, this morning the sky over Tokyo was so clear.

Miyazaki: That’s right.

—I feel a renewed appreciation for Tokyo at times like this.

Miyazaki: When we are too stressed we don’t notice it, but when the new green leaves come out above, it is wonderful to be able look up and enjoy their beauty. I think Oga-san looked closely at nature as he worked on this film. One interesting thing was that we put some dirt into a planter. All kinds of things grew. It turned into a very strange mini grass field. We counted quite a few types of plants. At first it was a planter with a fruit tree, but we ignored the fruit tree and became enthralled by the weeds growing from the dirt. We brought another handful of dirt from Nagano Prefecture. When strange plants started to grow, we wondered what they were. We thought them strange, but they were just ordinary weeds.

—Did you do any location scouting?

Miyazaki: We went location scouting behind the Nippon Animation studio just for a day or so. It was three of us: Oga-san, animation director Yoshiharu Satō, and myself. Also, Oga-san went several more times to take a look. He lives in Hino City, so he’s close by. Oga-san looked really carefully. So I think the background artists had a hard time. It takes a lot of effort to draw weeds.

—The way they grow is random.

Miyazaki: They’re poor grasses. When you draw a single plant it is pretty, but normally we just see weeds without paying them much attention. Those are the ones that are strangely nostalgic to us. Even now, when I see a sparrow’s gun, I want to blow into it to make a sound. Just after the war ended, we used to pick plantain weeds60 and deliver them to the authorities. They said they were baked into brown bread. It was an edible weed. We also ate chickweed. Weeds seem to grow strong and free. That might be what I like about them.

—I remember playing in the hollowed-out trunk of a tree in a nearby shrine.

Miyazaki: Old gnarled camphor trees have a bumpy bark that makes them easy to climb. The ones on the Tokyo University campus grow straight up and are hard to climb. But there are plenty of camphor trees like the one in Totoro. Close to Nippon Animation there was one old-looking tree that grew apart from the others. Camphor trees don’t live for thousands of years; they become quite splendid after several hundred years. Unfortunately, camphor trees don’t produce acorns. They just have small black seedpods. That’s why camphor trees don’t grow in Satsuki and Mei’s garden. In the scene when the seeds grow, I thought that if I tried to depict the plants with the least bit of exaggeration, it would no longer seem like Japan.

—Do you mean it would look like a jungle?

Miyazaki: No, that’s not it; it would become Europe. For example, the cat bus comes like a gust of wind. But if we make the gust of wind the cat bus, we shift away from Japan. There is no wind spirit in Japan, even though we do have a fellow who carries a bag and sends out wind from it. So if we make the cat bus into a wind spirit, suddenly it becomes something that isn’t Japanese.

Japan is modernized. The totoro are goblins of the transitional phase when Japan hadn’t become entirely modernized.

That’s what I felt strongly as I worked on this film. This is why I have no intention of returning to the ghostly spirits of Shigeru Mizuki;61 I can’t feel any affinity for those ghosts. As one born in the Shōwa period, it makes more sense to me to think that a cat goblin has turned into a bus. So to me, creating a story like Oshin62 or something from the Meiji period seems as distant as making a film with a foreign setting. I don’t feel any nostalgia when I hear about the old Magome post station on the Nakasendō route. What ate my heart are buildings that were built before the war and survived it and buildings of the early Shōwa period. Some try to show Japan with a spiritual medium from Osorezan63 or the world of people mired in the pathos of Ōtsu-e64 paintings. I have nothing to do with them. In that respect, unfortunately, I have become a modernized person. Kenji Miyazawa,65 who lived on the land in Iwate in northern Japan, longed for Europe somewhere in his heart. Though he had that longing, he wrote from that place just at the edge of his earth without leaving it. I felt sad when I read his Milky Way Railroad (Night on the Galactic Railroad; Ginga tetsudō no yoru). The main character in the novel seems like he would return home, take his shoes off, and step up into his house. Of course we have that kind of thing as well. I made Totoro wondering what kind of work it would be if I made something completely honest. I do want to state clearly that I didn’t make this film out of personal nostalgia for that time. I made it hoping that children would see it and then go out to run around the fields or pick up acorns. Or, though we only have a few such spaces left, that they would play in the thickets behind shrines, or become excited while peeking in the crawl spaces under their houses. That’s why I made this film.

Why I am attracted to plants

—From Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind on, your films consistently tackle the theme of wild nature and civilization, plants and people. When did you become so attracted to plant life?

Miyazaki: In childhood and youth, boys are fixated on girls and they don’t particularly like plants. I wasn’t especially fond of plants as a child. I only grew fond of them much later, after I turned thirty. But I do recall when I first noticed the beauty of a tree. I was enthralled by how magnificent the tree was. I was in about ninth grade.

Before then, I wasn’t particularly drawn to them even when walking in a wooded area. I thought that hyacinths were prettier than the flowers blooming in the fields. That was about it. I wasn’t especially impressed with spring’s new green leaves in my twenties; but in my thirties I had a period when I thought there was nothing more beautiful in this world than the new green leaves of zelkova trees. It was from that time that I became attracted to plants. Trees are so beautiful. Yet, when I went abroad and thought that some tree was wonderful or beautiful, there was always something different about my feelings.

For example, when I went to the giant redwood forests outside San Francisco, the forest floor was dry. And there were very few types of plants in the undergrowth. One could easily spend the night outside with just a blanket. In Japan, if you slept on a forest floor, centipedes or pill bugs would come out for sure. Flies, mosquitoes, and horseflies would be buzzing about. But in the redwood forests they weren’t there. It seemed a kind of false nature to me.

—Were those trees planted for reforestation?

Miyazaki: No, they weren’t. They were naturally growing trees. It may have been due to the climate, its humidity and temperature. When you go south, the scale is so large it’s hard to deal with. Then I find it most satisfying when I see a plant I know well.

In fact, when I went to Sweden on my first trip abroad, I thought the trees there were beautiful, but after I returned I thought they were monotonous. One weekend morning I was wandering around alone in Shakujii Park when no one was there. I was walking in the park at that hour because no one was at the Toei Animation studio that early, so I used to spend time there nearly every day after dropping my son off at nursery school. I thought the trees were so beautiful. I also realized that Japan is beautiful when there are no people around. [laughs] Japan became ugly because its population grew … That was when I understood. When reading Grimms’ fairy tales, I never could understand how people could sleep in the hollow of a tree. In Japan they would be covered by bugs. So many kinds of bugs would come out. A place full of those bugs, a place where so many trees and grasses grow in tiny, empty lots—that was nature to me. That is why I was always puzzled by the fairy tales.

That’s when I realized “Ah, I’m Japanese, after all.” I didn’t like many things in Japanese history, but I felt I understood something within myself.

—So for you it all started with being attracted to the trees and nature of Japan?

Miyazaki: Have you heard the term “broadleaf evergreen forest culture” promoted by Sasuke Nakao?

—No, I don’t know that term.

Miyazaki: This was a term coined by Nakao-san when I was about thirty years old. I received quite a shock when I read him.

There are very few peoples in the world who like fluffy, sticky rice. Just the Japanese, Yunnanese, and Nepalese. Also only a few like nattō, or sticky, fermented soybeans. The people who lived here before there was a Japan or a Japanese people were part of that cultural sphere in ancient times. This is why even now there is a dish like okowa (steamed rice with red beans) in Yunnan, and why the people of Bhutan look exactly like the Japanese. I felt greatly relieved when I realized that the country I had thought was confined to the Japanese archipelago—one with an insignificant history of the usually touted Genji and Hideyoshi Toyotomi66—was actually connected to the wider world beyond borders and ethnic groups. This is what the “broadleaf evergreen forest culture” is about.

I felt the clouds clear when I realized that even though Japanese may have committed various mistakes, we are not limited to that history—our history also includes the uninhibited clay vessels of the Jōmon period and the people who made them. It allowed me to look with more freedom than ever before at history as a whole: Japanese history, the way we are now, the folly of war, and many other things.

—Do you mean that Japan wasn’t as isolated a culture as you had thought?

Miyazaki: Yes, previously I had a really strong sense of it being closed. Looking at Japan with this new perspective, I was able to reassess myself, to figure out what sort of baggage I carried, and to recognize that I liked the stickiness of Japanese culture. The shape of my nose—which distressed me so much in my youth—is undoubtedly that of the Jōmon people. [laughs]

When I thought of these physical traits and the history I had learned at school—the postwar democracy that denied nearly all aspects of Japan, the anti-militarist backlash that taught me to say that Japan was a stupid, ignorant, fourth-rate country—I realized I had many bottled-up feelings inside. I was able to put all that aside, to suddenly liberate myself, and embrace the broadleaf evergreen forest culture. That’s when I understood why I preferred and felt calmed by zelkova trees rather than brush. [laughs]

I felt so liberated and so pleased when I found out that what flowed inside me was connected to the broadleaf evergreen forest. My culture went far beyond the idiotic Japanese who started the war, beyond Hideyoshi Toyotomi who invaded Korea, and beyond The Tale of Genji that I detested. It was then that I realized how valuable plants are and how important the issue of cultural climate is to us. If we were to destroy that climate, I would lose my last connection to being Japanese. The primeval forest of Hokkaido’s Shiretoko peninsula should be preserved, and I am furious at those who want to cut its trees down. But I don’t feel an intimate connection to it. It seems to be in a different cultural region. It feels like Europe or Siberia. There’s nothing wrong with that.

What I would like to see left alone are the woods and forests of beech trees, camphor trees, oak trees, and holly trees, though we don’t really have any broadleaf evergreen forests left in Japan. We had more of them in times past. It was because people honored the sakaki tree that was used for decoration. Japan’s original forests and woods are actually dark. When you enter into them, there’s something scary and exciting. It feels like an animal might be lurking just out of sight.

A world inhabited by spirits

—That is why Totoro lives inside a tree, in a forest that is dark and shadowy even during the day … Are totoro nocturnal? He was asleep during the day.

Miyazaki: Westerners tend to think of dark and light in opposition: light is good and dark is evil. I dislike this dichotomy. Although in Le Guin’s Earthsea books, she suggests that the dark is actually more powerful.

The Dark Crystal is similar. It simplistically divides things into a duality—one side is the Soviet Union and the other side is the United States. But I don’t think things can be treated with that kind of duality. For Japanese who don’t think that way, the gods are in the darkness. They may come out into the light at times, but they are usually deep in the forest or mountains. When a holy spot is created, the gods drop down onto it. That is why, in the shrines that are closest to their original form that still exist in Okinawa, though there are altars in the shrines, the image of the god is just a tree or a stone. And such a shrine isn’t in a bright, shining place, it’s in an overgrown dark area where the silence is deep—a butterfly might flit about, but it’s a bit eerie. When I went there with my children, they felt the eeriness and said it was scary. It felt as if something were there. This sense of dark awe is the sort of veneration that Japanese have toward certain forests and natural objects—in short, it’s an animistic primitive religion. Many places have a “forest that shouldn’t be entered.” Even people who are used to working in the mountains feel there is something there. They are suddenly overcome with fear and it becomes the custom to avoid certain places. These places exist. I don’t know what is there, but I think they are real. I’m not a believer in the occult, but the world is more than we can fathom with our five senses. This world doesn’t exist just for humans. So I think it is all right to have such things. This is why I think it’s a mistake to think about nature from the idea of efficiency, that forests should be preserved because they are essential for human beings …

I am concerned, because for me the deep forest is connected in some way to the darkness deep in my heart. I feel that if it is erased, then the darkness inside my heart would also disappear, and my existence would grow shallow.

I don’t go to worship at a shrine at New Year’s. It’s because I can’t believe that the gods are inside those gaudy shrines. It seems much more likely to me that the gods of the Japanese are deep in the mountains and far-off valleys. [laughs]

—That’s why Totoro is always snoring away.

Miyazaki: Or he’s casually dancing in a windstorm. I really wanted to have a typhoon scene.

—There is the gust of wind that comes up the night Satsuki and Totoro meet.

Miyazaki: That was the only way I could do it. Like having the house shake in the night wind. I wanted to show that sort of thing in a scene that everyone would have had an experience with. For example, when the house is creaking because of a typhoon and you wake up in the middle of the night to find your father nailing on the storm shutters outside on the veranda. Helping him out, you’re excited, and though your father says everything will be all right, you wonder if the house just might fly away … When you peek outside, the trees are bending way over and the leaves are flying around. Even the branches with green acorns come flying over. There was hole in the rain shutter, wasn’t there? I made a window that opened up like a “samurai window” for that purpose. But I couldn’t use it. Why couldn’t I use it? It was frustrating, but Mei’s eyes didn’t reach that high. [laughs]

—I suppose not. There wouldn’t be a window so low down. [laughs]

Miyazaki: If I could have done that well, then I could have depicted the night of the typhoon and made a really exciting movie.

—And when that night was over, you could have shown the cloudless blue sky the following day.

Miyazaki: And when you go outside, acorns would be strewn on the ground. If we could do that well, even just one scene, it would be a film with anticipatory excitement, what Paku-san calls making a clear impression of what one experiences. We may feel frightened during a typhoon, but that scared, excited feeling occupies a large place in our heart. In other words, it allows us to experience the meaning of the landscape or the climate on a much deeper level, rather than just recalling what someone said to us or how someone was mean to us. Actually, I think that the climate, landscape, weather, temperature, wind, rain, typhoons, and earthquakes that occur in Japan have especially strong meaning for Japanese people.

It is a delusion to think that we can ignore this, that we can raise children by installing air conditioners in rooms and saying “It stinks” when we go to the countryside, by putting air fresheners scented with fragrant olive (osmanthis) in our toilets so that children say, “That’s the toilet smell,” when they come across the scent of an actual fragrant olive plant. We can’t afford to be cut off from the earth. Totoro shows us a way of living that is not like that.

—Totoro is the spirit of nature, but says nothing. Had you decided from the start that Totoro would not speak?

Miyazaki: Yes. I was determined not to make him into Obake no Q-tarō (Q-tarō the Ghost).67 I thought he shouldn’t appear too much. I had also decided strictly against having scenes where Totoro sympathizes with Satsuki because she is sad. Satsuki is so endearing as she searches for Mei. Totoro can see that Mei is right over there, so he provides a service and takes her on the cat bus … That’s all that scene is about. He may not even be conscious of being of service to her.

—The cat bus is more human. It indicates the hospital name as its destination by flipping the signs, clatter clatter. [laughs]

Miyazaki: I made the sign clatter in a familiar way so that younger children watching the film would feel some relief. I knew that to see “Mei” on the destination sign would absolutely give them a sense of relief. I wanted to end on a note of good feeling. The ending would have been too quick if they hadn’t been reassured until the moment Mei was found on the road. When the cat bus comes with its destination sign clattering, the audience knows that Mei will be found. Then they also think that the mother will be all right as well. This is a directorial decision. So I thought it was all right to include happy music in that scene.

—I understand that in the initial synopsis, Satsuki meets Totoro and lends him her umbrella. When she wakes up the next day she finds the umbrella at the entrance to the house along with a thank-you gift of acorns. Why did you change that?

Miyazaki: Yes, I did make a storyboard of the return of the umbrella. But that would make Totoro understand things too much. There is no way he would understand lending and borrowing. And there is no way Totoro would have an aversion to getting wet in the rain.

—Because he is an animal.

Miyazaki: Right. And rain helps plants grow. Since he lives as the lord of the forest, Totoro can hear the joyous voices of the plants. There is no way such a being would dislike the rain. Especially as it’s rain falling during the rainy season. When we think of him enjoying the plop, plop sound of the raindrops on the leaf on his head, he wouldn’t be thankful for having been lent an umbrella. I realized that we had a problem. Then I thought that he would be happy hearing the louder plop, plop of the raindrops as they hit the umbrella. Totoro thinks the umbrella was some kind of wonderful musical instrument. Satsuki has given him a musical instrument, and he has no concept of returning it. Instead, he thinks it is best to give some acorns to reciprocate.

This shows how stories aren’t always completely finalized at the start.

—Children really loved that bus stop scene in the movie theaters.

Miyazaki: It made me very happy. That scene has very little action and it’s a difficult one. Satsuki is surprised but she’s also excited.

—The children must have been sharing that feeling.

Miyazaki: I thought about who should meet Totoro first, and decided it should be Mei, who doesn’t shrink from things. Satsuki may be tough-minded, but even she would have been shocked to find a huge creature standing next to her in the rain. So it seemed better to have Mei meet him first, then Satsuki. When Satsuki is quietly standing alongside Totoro, the cat bus appears, ruffles things up, and leaves. So it’s not necessary for her to be frightened when the cat bus appears all of a sudden. But when we put in the roar of the wind, it became scary. I realized that was more true to the scene. So it became difficult to put in the music composed by Hisaishi-san.

—Was there some music?

Miyazaki: Yes, the cat bus theme tone—ta-ta-ta-ta—was supposed to be inserted there. When we put it in, it turned into a jolly, silly cat, a foreign cat.

I had always imagined that it would come with the wind, riding on the wind. But it was among large trees. There was no way to have the wind blow. I didn’t want to kill that background art’s depth by putting in more cels, so I had decided not to have the wind blow. But, curiously, the roar of the wind didn’t sound strange when we actually put it in. [laughs]

Between reality and dream …

—The fantastic scene of the soot sprites crossing over to the woods at night is very interesting as it dovetails effortlessly into reality.

Miyazaki: It’s as if I’m creating two entirely different types of stories. I thought about the ways in which the appearance of Totoro and the depiction of nature would mesh. I had some doubts, but I was convinced that if I showed that house, that forest and nature surrounding it, and those children clearly at the start, it would seem too normal for an odd creature to appear. And if it were normal for any creature to appear at that point, the whole thing would just seem like a big fantasy. That’s why it was important to depict how they lived in detail at the beginning. In the latter half, that could be skipped over. That made our calculation of the number of seconds required for the scene go way off schedule. [laughs]

—Is that what happened?

Miyazaki: I didn’t think it would require so many seconds for the first night after they moved in.

—So Part A expanded?

Miyazaki: It wasn’t that it expanded, it just needed that much time.

—This film doesn’t have much of a plot line compared to your other works.

Miyazaki: No, it doesn’t.

—The climax is when the little girl almost gets lost … I thought you took quite a risk with that. There could have been more of a story there. What was your intention?

Miyazaki: I had no doubts at all from the beginning. I even thought I created quite a bit of a story. It felt like I could even have made a film that would excite children just from one night’s typhoon.

—Is that because you could make them feel moved enough?

Miyazaki: No, that’s not it. The night of a typhoon is in itself something unusual within normal life, something that creates its own excitement. So if that can’t be made interesting, this type of movie can’t succeed. And this relates to what I said earlier about not having to use tricks. Satsuki and Mei meet Totoro, but the scenes of the bus stop where Satsuki meets him and inside the tree where Mei first meets him are basically presented so that it is unclear whether they were real or a dream. Of course, I think they really happened.

—The part where the tree grows when they do the dondoko odori dance is the same way.

Miyazaki: That was, well, a radiological mutation of the tree …[laughs]

—As I watched, I wondered if it was an expression of the largeness of the life force inside the seed, or a dream the tree itself was dreaming.

Miyazaki: [laughs] [Though he laughed, Miyazaki-san didn’t say anything. It seemed he wanted viewers to make their own interpretations.]

—I like the phrase, “It was a dream, but it wasn’t a dream.”

Miyazaki: It might do for Satsuki to utter such an overly explanatory phrase, but I wondered if Mei should follow so closely behind. I think I should have had Satsuki say, “It wasn’t a dream,” once, then have her say it again, and then have Mei imitate her and say it. [laughs] There’s no need to understand everything. When I’m asked what a totoro is, I don’t know myself. Talking with Joe Hisaishi, we agonized over the music—it would not do to emphasize the mystical. And it would also not fit to create undue affinity with Totoro, as if he were merely an ordinary tanuki raccoon dog that had just shown up there. That’s why Hisaishi-san’s minimalist music was best. If it were more mysterious, then it would be supernatural. What I thought was perfect was that he includes sounds we’ve heard somewhere and recognize, but still gives us the feeling that something is a bit different.

What was strange was the music for the scene when Totoro appears standing beside Satsuki. The music—with a seven beat basic rhythm—was playing all along. I asked to have some of it removed as it was a bit too much.

So Hisaishi-san removed a little bit each time during the mixing. He counted, “one, two, three, four, five, six, seven, huh” to remove it. And then counted again to insert it. The result turned out well. When that was played with the film, it was perfect. It was an amazing fit.

When Satsuki looks up, there’s no rhythm, and when Totoro appears the rhythm comes on. Once in a while we’re lucky and that kind of thing happens. I liked the music in that section. It didn’t get in the way, and it didn’t determine a certain character, yet it is strange and mystical, but not too mysterious, and it’s oddly familiar … That scene turned out great with the sound and music added.

Hisaishi-san had a lot of concerns about the music. He thought it needed to be light. It was only after completing the film that I was able to state definitively that it didn’t have to be light. During the process, I told Hisaishi-san that it wasn’t necessary to have the music so light, that it wouldn’t work to put music in everywhere indiscriminately, and had him redo some of the music in places. There are several places that I thought, “Shoot, if only I knew more about traditional dance music, things could have been better.” With music you really can’t tell if it will work until you actually put it in.

—Nausicaä says that gunships cut through the wind, but her mehve rides on the wind. I was surprised by that image. But in Totoro he becomes the wind. It seems like Totoro was having so much fun, twirling around and flying in the wind.

Miyazaki: If that scene were in an action story, I could have pushed it more. But he’s just flying as the wind. I couldn’t push it anymore than that. If I had pushed it, it would have expanded too much, into a larger scene, so I restrained it a lot. It would be different if he were going somewhere on the wind. Without that kind of purpose, I felt it was okay to finish the scene with, “Hey, we’ve become the wind!” That’s all there is, nothing more.

—Just that alone is a dreamy experience for Satsuki and Mei.

Miyazaki: I do regret something. You know how when the wind blows across water little ripples form with the gusts? Oga-san and I often talked about wishing we could do that. He said, “If you gave me three times the time, I could do it for you.” And in the same way, when I would say it would be wonderful if we could draw the camphor tree as if it were really swaying in the wind, he would reply, “If I had three times as long, I could do it.”

—There were scenes where the top of the camphor tree was swaying.

Miyazaki: We were thinking of something else, not that kind of overlap. We gave up on it. We figured out how to do it, but would artists of Oga-san’s caliber be able to draw the frames? The issue becomes how many frames are needed for the animator to draw the moving tree and then draw the leaves, and then to have the shimmering movement as it changes color. We were convinced that we could show the movement in three frames and draw the shimmering in one frame. If we did that we could show the leaves waving on the branches and the light dappling through. But there was no way we could have enough time to do that.

When they cross the ridge, if the ears of rice are out, flowers should be blooming by then too. If we drew that, we wouldn’t be able to show movement. We made it just a carpet of green, but that kind of thing is frustrating. Oga-san and I would discuss how wonderful it would be to show that when the wind blows the rice stalks bend and the wind creates a depression in the field as it blows by. If it’s a river, the ripple of waves on the surface … but that would take three times as long to do. [laughs]

—In Panda! Go Panda!, the pandas appear all of a sudden in Mimiko’s daily life. What do you think of the difference in that regard with Totoro?

Miyazaki: I don’t think it’s that different. The Kenji Miyazawa story I like best is Donguri to yamaneko (The Acorns and the Wildcat). Even after reading it, I couldn’t figure out what the wildcat was. And it wasn’t necessary to know. When I saw the illustration, I didn’t like it at all. I thought it was wrong to have such a small wildcat. In my mind I had imagined a huge, two-meter tall wildcat just standing there, looking off in the distance, with small acorns running around screeching at its feet. When I thought of the wildcat vacantly standing there, I fell in love with that world. Actually, I was quite disappointed when I first saw a fox. Doesn’t it seem that a creature that can bewitch people should be larger? I thought it wasn’t possible that a creature smaller than a medium-sized dog could bewitch people. It was the same when I saw a real tanuki raccoon dog as well. When you hear of a raccoon dog, doesn’t it seem like a large, dazed animal? Having often heard as a child from my mother about raccoon dogs and foxes bewitching people, and about foxes that turn into humans and ride on horses and are caught as they try to eat deep fried tofu, I assumed they were much larger animals. But they weren’t.

That was why when I made Panda! Go Panda! I made sure they were huge and just dazedly standing there. I am Japanese after all. We have the term korikō or “being clever” (on a small scale). We also have the term taigu (great fool) for incredible stupidity. I think Japanese people like a bottomless, enormous foolishness, especially in people who become important. These great fools are able to enfold those people who are scuttling about concerned with minutiae. For example, Takamori Saigō68 gives off that kind of air. I like that kind of dazed benightedness that wafts around enormous foolishness. This is how Totoro came to be that way.

—When he grins broadly, without any dialogue, viewers feel a sense of liberation just from watching him.

Miyazaki: Unlike the foreign countries, where people may argue about whether Christ ever laughed or not, Japanese gods are basically laughing gods. It’s like the weather in Japan: there may be some harsh times, but basically the sun smiles on us. When I was young I hated that. I sought something with greater severity.

—Japanese gods are more commonplace. They seem to be no different from humans in terms of their emotions.

Miyazaki: They can become wrathful. That’s why they must be soothed; and when they are soothed, they become smiling, gentle gods. I like these gods better. It doesn’t seem to me that Japanese people want a god who saves their souls. People talk about where they will go when they die, but rather than paradise or heaven, it seems more fitting that they actually will be reborn into the mountains or earth, or trees growing nearby. That’s why I prefer burial of the body. When my mother was cremated, I strongly felt I would rather have her body buried. Then, if flowers bloomed above her grave plot I could think, “Look, my mother has turned into these flower blossoms.” It seemed a waste to turn her body into mere carbon dioxide and carbon. I thought it would be better to have it become fertilizer for trees, grasses, or insects, to became a fragment of things that would live on … What am I talking about? [laughs]

—In the story, there is no explanation at all about Totoro or the cat bus, so it would be fine if someone thought they were gods, or beast spirits, or goblins. The premise that a cat-beast spirit saw a bus and thought it would be fun to imitate it is really interesting.

Miyazaki: The cat bus used to be just a cat ghost. He saw a bus and thought it looked like fun. Totoro, too, learned about Jōmon clay pots from the Jōmon people, and is likely spinning tops imitating an Edo period boy who was playing with them. [laughs] Totoro live for 3,000 years, so to him these are things he learned just a short while ago. It may be that Kanta’s grandmother met Totoro when she was a child, crying as she walked along the woods after being scolded by her parents. Totoro may think that Granny as a little girl and Mei are the same girl.

—The cat bus has twelve legs; how did you depict its running?

Miyazaki: We talked about how its legs should move in sequence, like a centipede’s. I left it up to Katsuya Kondō to create the movement and had him show me his drawings every now and then. There is a bit of a strain; probably it’s best to draw it in one frame. When animals walk, in actuality their shoulders move ahead. But on the cat bus the shoulder joint is stuck on too tight, so we couldn’t show this movement. It’s much easier to fake the way a fictional character runs. It’s much more difficult to show Mei and Satsuki, who are real children, running.

To create the world of children

—What is it like for Satsuki and Mei to meet Totoro?

Miyazaki: Satsuki and Mei are saved just by Totoro’s existence. Just by his being. He helps when they are looking for Mei, but I thought it wouldn’t work if he went searching with them.

—It’s enough to know “There was once a Treasure Island.”

Miyazaki: That’s right. It’s “Laputa really existed after all.” [laughs] There’s no purpose to it; Treasure Island exists and Totoro exists. Because of his existence, Satsuki and Mei aren’t isolated and helpless. I think that is sufficient. By creating this kind of film I have come to realize what is in my core. Such as what I like. I recalled all sorts of things about my own childhood, when my children were young, what my now-grown nieces and nephews did when they were little. Because of these recollections, I didn’t have to fret over what the children would do when they went outside.

One example is when my younger son—he’s probably forgotten all about it—went shopping with his mother when he was in nursery school. It was a windy day, and when they came home my wife told me, “I wonder if our son is a genius.” He had said to her, “Today the beads of air bumped into us.” This was the reality that he felt. I’m sure my son has forgotten it, as has my wife. At that time I thought, that’s the kind of thing a child would say. But I remembered that in creating the scene where the firewood that Satsuki is carrying is blown away by the gust of wind. And I smashed beads of air there. For some strange reason, that kind of phrase remains in my head. My Neighbor Totoro was created from gathering bits like that. When my mother was in the hospital and I returned home from school to an empty house, I definitely experienced loneliness …

—Satsuki seems more mature than an actual fourth grader.

Miyazaki: She was forced to become more mature, as children do in her situation. Fourth grade is about the time when a child graduates from watching Doraemon. It’s the time of life when you become the main character in your own story. There’s a children’s story called Boku wa Ōsama (I Am King) that young children love. Actually, the story is about oneself. A stupid king appears and enjoys reading it, saying, “This king is really stupid,” as he giggles. But actually he’s reading about himself. In the same way, it is in fourth grade that a child starts to think, “Rather than this kind of person, I’d like to be that kind, or I wish things were that way.” It’s about then that children start to like someone. I think age ten is one touch point of separation. If you were to ask me if Satsuki is supposed to be ten years old, I’m not sure how I would answer … but ten-year-olds do live with these concerns. At least I think so. A ten-year-old can do kitchen chores. I did. I also cleaned house, fired up the bath, and cooked.

—I heard from a staff member that you gave a lecture on walking to the in-betweeners during the production. Is that true?

Miyazaki: It wasn’t about walking, it was about running.

—What did you tell them?

Miyazaki: Their drawings weren’t of running. Everyone had memorized the formula but it wasn’t real running. Running with six frames in a two-frame setup wasn’t yet the norm when I entered Toei Animation. There were various opinions—some asserted that a six-frame sequence was best and others thought using five frames was better for running. The Yasuo Ōtsuka group, including Paku-san, thought six frames was correct and they insisted on it. At that time I was lectured by Ōtsuka-san as to how to show running in six frames of two frames each. The three drawings each had meaning and purpose: one was the coiled spring, the next was the stretch, and then the kick up. It only looks like running when these three factors are combined, so three pictures are needed of these three poses. I was convinced by his explanation. But that’s not to say one should produce this pattern by rote under all circumstances. I applied this theory in my own way to figure out how to shift the pattern when a little child runs or when a bad guy runs. In doing so, I came up with my own theory, because I figured out that drawing this kind of pose would not always work, for a variety of reasons. I thought this had become common practice, but realized it hadn’t at all.

People were drawing in a set pattern without knowing why, just thinking that this was the only way to draw running. Even people I had instructed as new hires had already forgotten the reasoning behind the method.

How to determine whether to have both the upper and lower body move, when movement should or shouldn’t be put in—that is what I instructed them on. But just one lecture wasn’t enough for them to remember it.

—Did you do the running for Satsuki and Mei?

Miyazaki: No, I just explained the basics of the kinds of running to our animators and told them what they should observe when they wanted to draw characters running. When you want hectic movement, of course just shifting the images will express that movement. But rather than that, I told them to think about whether it would be better this way or another, and what sort of meaning a specific technique imparts to a character’s movement. There are three basic poses that come from this way of thinking. But even a slight mistake in assembling them makes the character’s running look unnatural, as if they are stiff or rattling along. I corrected a lot of running sequences. But this ends up with the director having also to correct the key and in-between animation and insert reference poses. So we wound up with some truly miserable-looking frames.

—Watching first and second graders at a primary school, I sometimes wonder how they can move around so much and not get tired. And they’re so quick.

Miyazaki: Yes. Children aren’t conscious of wanting to run. They’re only aware of wanting to get somewhere quickly. In order to go quickly they naturally end up running. So, when you send a little child on an errand and tell him, “Don’t run,” as he is replying, “Okay,” he’s already running. [laughs] He thinks of moving quickly and does so without intentionally running. This film is not pulled along by a story; it is the frames themselves that must be expressive. In that sense, the abilities of the animators are insufficient. They are grossly insufficient!

—They aren’t accustomed to doing it that way—few stories require that of animators.

Miyazaki: That’s not all. There are too many animators who aren’t interested in that way of expression. Many of them didn’t enter into this field to draw everyday occurrences; they want to forget about reality and go somewhere else. Like explosions. It didn’t bother me when I was working on action dramas, but it bothered me greatly when working on Totoro—not only with Satsuki and Mei but in many other respects as well. Children don’t run at the same rhythm. When you look at children in reality, you can tell so much. It’s all helter-skelter. When you watch children as they walk home chatting with their friends you can tell that they don’t walk as they talk back and forth like in Sally the Witch. They turn backwards, wander off to the side, and this goes on without any break. I told the animators over and over again to watch from the window at the way children pass by.

Satsuki and Mei

—Do you have any daughters?

Miyazaki: No, I don’t. I have two sons. And my own siblings are males, all four of us.

—Why did you decide to make this a story of two sisters?

Miyazaki: That’s because I am a man. If it were a story of boys, it would have been Kanta and his brother. That wouldn’t be the same.

—It would become more rough-hewn?

Miyazaki: No, that’s not it … It would be more pitiful and I couldn’t have made it. It would overlap too much with my own childhood. So I didn’t want to make that kind of film. For example, I didn’t have a close relationship with my mother like Satsuki. I was overly self-conscious, and my mother was that way too. When I went to see her in the hospital, I couldn’t rush to hug her. It’s natural for Satsuki to feel a bit shy and not go directly to her mother’s side. Then, what would Satsuki’s mother do? She might brush Satsuki’s hair or something. That’s one way she could show physical affection. That is what gives Satsuki support. There must have been that kind of consideration. Actually this was a real occurrence. Kihara-kun, in production, told me about a woman he had heard about.

Her mother was ill and she couldn’t do anything for her child. But on her sickbed she did brush her child’s hair as she talked to her. And the daughter felt her mother’s love with this gesture. It wouldn’t be natural for the mother to give her daughter expansive hugs, like it would be for Americans. It would be even more unlikely when the girl is a fourth grader. So for Satsuki, the ritual of having her hair brushed is very meaningful. Mei, on the other hand, can crawl into her mother’s lap and feel the warmth of her body. Mei is still young enough to hug her mother.

—I was so moved by the gentle and free movement of the three on the bicycle, overflowing with relief, as they make their way home after visiting the hospital. It was a wonderful scene, their expressions full of the joy they felt inside. I’m not sure if I should even ask you, the director, about this … There is scene in which Satsuki, unable to contain her emotions, starts crying. I’d like to ask about that scene.

Miyazaki: Initially, I didn’t intend to have her cry so much. I thought of Satsuki as a girl who could keep her emotions under control. After completing part B, I looked at the relationship between Satsuki and Mei. Mei is guileless and doesn’t think deeply about things, so she’s not gloomy. But Satsuki gets depressed. Why? Because she is trying too hard to be good. If she could recognize that she is trying too hard, Satsuki would be able to feel better. If she fails to do this, she will turn into a delinquent. It was only after the storyboards had been done for part B that I realized I had to let this girl’s emotions explode somewhere. I was already starting part C.

—She was opening the rain shutters, making meals and box lunches, and taking care of her little sister.

Miyazaki: After part C, when I started on part D, I thought Satsuki would reach her limit … But I didn’t like the idea of her slamming cooking pots around. I have the same kind of memory. For a child to have to do kitchen work while his mother is sick in bed is not a moving story. It’s daily life. If it’s once in a while when your mother is sick for a day, that’s fine. But it’s tough to do it on a regular basis.

—And she’s been doing it all along for half a year or so.

Miyazaki: Her father is doing some as well … So I thought Satsuki couldn’t be redeemed unless she yelled and cried once. That is why her mother in the hospital says she feels sorry for Satsuki. I thought unless I could understand Satsuki’s feelings, she would become warped and turn into a delinquent … This is why in the pictures during the ending credits I had Satsuki feeling relieved with her mother at home and playing normally with the other children. Their faces look similar so you can’t tell which one is Satsuki, but that’s fine. And it is better to have Mei, who has been the little sister bossed around by her older sister, find a younger kid to pull into her play.

This is why I intentionally didn’t show the sisters together with Totoro at the end.

If they had stayed in that place with Totoro, those girls wouldn’t have been able to return to the world of human reality. I think it’s fine if they never see Totoro again.

—They may make a snowman and totoros may be watching.

Miyazaki: They may watch, but that’s all right too. Basically, it was only when I reached the end that I realized what I had to do for the last shots. It was only then that I was finally able to see the entire work. It was then that I decided there would be no sequel. It was enough for them to have met Totoro just during one part of their lives.

The scene that concerned me most

—It seems many people recall their own childhoods when they watch My Neighbor Totoro.

Miyazaki: Shortly after I moved to Tokorozawa, a neighbor family’s child was thought to have fallen into the nearby river. The parents jumped into the river and the entire neighborhood was all upset. It was a filthy river, a drainage ditch, but everyone joined in an exhaustive search. Then, the child appeared out of nowhere; he had just been playing somewhere else. This sort of thing happens. Suddenly, ordinary life just flies out the window.

Once when we went to a festival my little brother didn’t make it home with the rest of us, and fearing that he had been taken by someone, we all split up to search for him, losing sight of one another in the process. We realized he had gotten lost. Nowadays everyone has telephones and kids don’t get lost as often. I still recall how I felt when I thought we might not find him, when we split up and searched in the dark for him at the festival. When we found him, he was crying, clinging to the sleeve of an old woman he didn’t know. That old woman hadn’t known what to do so she had walked around with him. [laughs] It’s episodes like this that make up this film. There’s no need for a plot. I wish I could have made it a ninety-minute film. If I could have, I would have expanded the section on Satsuki and Mei’s everyday lives, rather than the Totoro section—that would make it the story of Satsuki and Mei, not the story of Totoro.

There’s a part when Satsuki and Mei are asleep after making a telephone call. That’s the part I had trouble with for a while. I drew Satsuki crouching in the kitchen, feeling sad, but it was totally wrong. I hadn’t felt that way. When my mother was in the hospital, only our maid was at home—do we call them helpers now? Well, sometimes maids and children don’t get along very well. Sometimes they are enemies. In the morning the maid would yank the covers off me to wake me up, and she was heartless. She was only eighteen or twenty years old, and she had to deal with these brats who wouldn’t listen to her, so she must have been mad. My older brother came home from school one day after second period and said, “The dog’s been taken away.” This was the dog I had been caring for. I recalled how I felt then. I felt so nauseated I couldn’t stay on my feet. More than feeling sad or sorry for the dog, I just didn’t know what to do. Had my parents been there, I’m sure I would have begged them to do something, but they weren’t around. The maid couldn’t care less. With the dog gone, she wouldn’t have to take care of it. When I try to think of what I did then, I can’t remember at all … Probably, I went to sleep.

I talked it over with Tsugiko Kubota-san, the children’s literature author who wrote the novelization of My Neighbor Totoro. When we asked, “What do you think children do at those times?” she, too, said she thought they would fall asleep. And when I drew Satsuki lying down and falling asleep, it made perfect sense to me.

I thought, Mei wouldn’t go to sleep right away, and she would have finished crying by the time she returned home. She plays with a toy from habit, but she isn’t fully engaged with it. Then she gets tired and falls asleep …

When I resolved that scene, I suddenly realized what I must have done in my own childhood.

—You understood what happened in a blank part of your life. [laughs]

Miyazaki: That’s right. I realized that’s what must have happened. I probably fell asleep in any old room. I must have fallen asleep on the spot without caring about blankets or a pillow. I don’t think the maid would have brought a pillow or placed a blanket over me. That’s why I like that scene in Totoro so much. I was so happy that I was able to coax out that feeling.

When I drew Satsuki asleep on the floor and asked Oga-san what he thought, he said, “It must feel good to take a nap in a spot like that.” [laughs]

Parents can misunderstand their children. They think that children are able to fall asleep with no cares at all even in the most stressful of times and conclude that children are innocent. In reality, the children are not without care—they are so upset they can’t stay standing up, so they go to sleep as a self-defense mechanism. It’s when they are the most troubled that they fall asleep. Children who hate school and refuse to go, come home and fall asleep; it’s when they are so troubled and full of stress that they sleep … When I finally understood this behavior, I felt I was able to comprehend a lot of things a bit better than before.

Moreover, Mei sleeps holding an ear of corn. At that angle, we can’t see the corn, but it wouldn’t have worked to draw her facing us. If we saw her face, and it was a dejected face, it would become false. I loved being able to overcome some of my misconceptions and create that scene.

—I hadn’t noticed that she was holding an ear of corn. How unobservant of me.

Miyazaki: You can understand, though, can’t you? When I went to a seaside summer school and saw a sea urchin for the first time in my life, I desperately wanted to show this fascinating thing to my sick mother. I stored the sea urchin in the crawl space under the floor. When I think of it now, I’m sure my mother must have seen sea urchins long before then. [laughs] Naturally, the sea urchin spoiled before I could take it home, and I remember being disappointed. I just wanted to share my surprise and joy—it hadn’t occured to me that my mother might already know about sea urchins.

This is the first ear of corn that Mei has picked—she’s forgotten that Granny had picked it for her. She’s convinced that if she can deliver it to her mother, her mother will eat it and get well. So corn becomes a key word for Mei. “My mom will get well if I give her the corn, Granny said so.” What’s more, this corn was picked by Mei, whom Mother loves so much.

—It’s bound to work a cure.

Miyazaki: I don’t know if she thought it through that far, but at least she is sure her mother will come home to her if she can give her the corn.

—Her older sister is despondent and crying.

Miyazaki: That is the first realization for Mei that something is wrong. Seeing Satsuki cry, she thinks her mother is in peril. That is why Mei thinks she has to go to her mother no matter what … This is something so valuable. I think we’ve all experienced this once or twice when we were children.

—What about the word “Mother” on the corn?

Miyazaki: Satsuki must have engraved it with her fingernail as she sat on the branch of the tree next to the hospital. Mei is still too young to be able to write.

—In the climactic scene of searching for Mei, the background artwork gives a wonderful sense of urgency as sunset turns to twilight. How did you direct the light for this film?

Miyazaki: I’m always aware of the light. Unless there’s light, the screen won’t hold viewers’ interest. In Totoro over one-fourth of the running time consists of the last sequence when the sun goes gradually from midday to the west, then sunset, and dusk to night. It must have been difficult for Oga-san.

We wondered if it should have gotten a little darker as we were working on it, but that would have been hard to do. It would have been easy to have a bright red sunset, but instead we toned down the sunset in the preceding shots.

When they leave the hospital to go home, the sun is starting to set, so the background should have strong colors. Since the story is set in Japan, we had to depict the background art and light with a sure hand. Because all the viewers are familiar with the sun’s rays in Japan, we couldn’t make any mistakes. I don’t think we did everything perfectly. But having forced ourselves to avoid garish colors, we were lucky to be able to make the changes in the color and light vivid enough for the final act of the film. We joked that it was the light of summer in the northern highlands. [laughs] “There’s no humidity, so it must be summer in Akita.”

—As with the light, other impressions of the weather also play a major role: falling rain, blowing wind, summer days, a silvery moonlit night. Is this because children are sensitive to weather?

Miyazaki: Isn’t the world like that? [laughs] That is all. It’s just showing that there are sunny days and cloudy days and sudden evening downpours and nights when the wind blows. People live feeling that kind of weather and temperature on their skin.

Kanta’s Granny took to Satsuki and Mei because she loved them for being able to see the soot sprites even though they had come from the city. She realized that they were not just unusual city kids, but children whose eyes were unclouded and clear. Such children notice the blueness of the sky, the acorns on the ground, the little flowers blooming at the roadside. And this is true of all children throughout the country.

—We may see more adults and children gazing in awe at the immensity of the clouds drifting in the sky after seeing Castle in the Sky. Or noticing new plant leaves and the little flowers on weeds as they walk along the road after seeing Totoro.

Miyazaki: I experienced tremendous happiness as I was making this film.

—Miyazaki-san, please continue your efforts to make wonderful animation films for children. Thank you.
