Earth’s Environment as Metaphor

Interviewers: Tetsuji Yamamoto, Jun’ichi Takahashiiichiko No. 33, No. 34, Japan Belier Art Center, October 20, 1994; January 20, 1995 issues

Jun’ichi Takahashi: born 1950; professor, Waseda University; author of Sensō to Bōryoku no Keifugaku (Genealogy of War and Violence), etc.

Tetsuji Yamamoto: born 1948; former professor, Shinshū University; author of Michel Foucault no Shikou Taikei (Michel Foucault’s Systems of Thought), etc.

Life forms and ohmu

Yamamoto: Our subject for this issue is cultural studies of earth’s environment—in this discussion we will consider the earth’s environment from the point of view of culture. Recently, Miyazaki-san finished drawing Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind. Reading it, I was moved to tears. This makes me want to ask Miyazaki-san about his views on the environment. People find it very difficult to understand environmental problems from discussions about the ways in which the environment is being destroyed or the fact that the earth is facing these sorts of changes. But when these issues are depicted as in Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind, they are forced to think about them in a symbolic manner. I am now urging my students to read Nausicaä because by reading it we are made to think and feel much more deeply than we do when we listen to what scholars say. I think we can approach these problems with real faithfulness to the issues from this vantage point.

What I want to ask right off is about the ohmu. I was extremely moved by them. What are ohmu, where did you come across their existence, and what are you expressing in the form of the ohmu? Could you speak about these points?

Miyazaki: I don’t recall those aspects as it was over ten years ago when I started drawing the story. What I can say is that in my childhood I found Tarzan movies thrilling. When Tarzan called out “Aaaaaayaaaaahaaaaah!” a whole herd of elephants would come to him. Actually they were Asian elephants and not African elephants, but from that image I felt as though Tarzan had the strongest creatures in the entire world at his beck and call—and I was filled with longing. I think we were all like that. When I became an elementary school pupil, I learned that elephants can be killed by machine guns and I recall being very dejected. I had assumed that machine gun bullets would bounce off elephants. In popular culture this attraction toward the strongest being appears repeatedly, even though it may change form. In the work Shōnen Keniya (Kenya Boy) by Sōji Yamakawa, a snake named Dana appears. I don’t know why, but it just slowly slithers out. And I was relieved when it did.

Related to that, there was a film called The NeverEnding Story. It was somewhat changed from the original story. A dragon appears and has a very obvious face. You can tell what it is thinking from its face. This was totally uninteresting. For us something is far more likely to become an object of our longing when we don’t know what it is thinking. The more we humans anthropomorphize something and make it an easy target for empathy, the less interesting it becomes. From the beginning we seem to have a longing for a presence or a power that is far greater than ourselves and not easily understood, a presence beyond our current framework or whose origins are prehistoric. This longing isn’t unique to me, but rather is what we all feel, a memory repeated over and over from our ancestors. When I think of myself and the way I think of nature, I seem to want to comprehend it in such a way. I don’t know why, but nature exists as an incredible force, as something huge, far exceeding our own little good or evil ways. This is the view of nature that we have.

Watching The NeverEnding Story, I feel the filmmaker’s perspective is not the same as mine. It’s uninteresting, like the dragon. There’s also a tortoise that has a face like a drunkard’s. Had its eyes been depicted as unfathomable holes, I would have been moved when the huge creature crawls out of the rock. But when I saw the face that appeared, looking like a drunken Disneyland doll, my thought was that the filmmaker’s view of nature was so stunted I couldn’t take it. It made me lose interest in watching the film.

Takahashi: It seems to me that among Europeans, or in European culture, the idea of nature untouched by human hands is unthinkable. They can’t even conceive of raw nature.

Miyazaki: I don’t know if that is true of all of Europe. If you go to Ireland, it may not be that way. But what comes to mind when we think of European-style nature is the Tyrol region. I don’t like it at all. Let’s keep this a forest, let’s make this farmland, let’s have people live here, let’s build roads here. They’ve made it exactly as planned. When I went there, it was so boring. Walking there was absolutely uninteresting.

Yamamoto: Do those thoughts relate to the form of the ohmu?

Miyazaki: I think anyone who collected insects as a child had the experience of looking at the bumps on a cicada’s head and wondering why its shape is so interesting. They have three simple eyes—small red spots. They look practically like jewels. The wonder one experiences in that moment is extraordinary. And the amazing colors of the dragonflies like the large oniyanma and the yellowish ginyanma. These are among the first awesome things children encounter. Looking at the larvae of beetles or the curve of the back of a crayfish, or the spaceshiplike shape of an ant, we feel like we’ve peeked into some of the secrets of the world. When I was in elementary school and learned from my teacher that spiders have eight eyes, I was so shocked that my world seemed to crumble. I wondered, “How can they see with so many eyes?” I made the ohmu from a collection of all these experiences.

When I want to have a huge creature appear that represents the birth of another ecosystem, the world in which reptiles and mammals live doesn’t work because it is too familiar to us. Technically as well, no matter how we change the form of reptiles or mammals, it is just a collection of familiar parts. It seemed to me that combining insects and arthropods could more easily result in creatures we have no way of recognizing. I wanted this form to resist empathy. There are many people who don’t like bugs, so I thought bugs were just right to express a contrasting ecosystem. I also wanted to use the term “writhing.” A friend of mine borrowed a prewar book full of censored words. It was by Rampo Edogawa, but there were so many blanked-out words that I couldn’t tell what was going on in the story. The term writhing seemed awfully full of squirming, so it stayed in my mind. The origin of the word ohmu is a mixture of the words for the king’s worm, the sandworm from Dune, and Daijirō Morohoshi’s Buddhist term “ohm.” It is written with the character for king, and because it has many legs the second character consists of three bugs. The rest was how to show it as a large bug. I considered many forms, wondering, “What will this guy look like when he becomes an adult?”

Takahashi: Are ohmu forever in larval form?

Miyazaki: I thought of various ways to treat them, even wondering if one day they might all suddenly become adults. But I thought that would push me into a trap of my own making, so I didn’t pursue it for this story. I thought it more fitting to think of ohmu as beings whose lives end in the larval form.

The forest and the Sea of Decay

Yamamoto: A new world opens up with the ohmu going into the Sea of Decay. What is the condition under which fungi and bacteria, which are a cross between plants and animals, overrun the area? Is the image one of the environment decaying with mold?

Miyazaki: No, the term “Sea of Decay,” a rotted sea, comes from a place that exists at the base of the Crimean Peninsula. It’s called Sivash where the sea has ebbed away and become a marshland. Apparently they also call this area the Sea of Decay. When I first read this term it made a strong impression on me. Mold suggests rot to humans. Just as the term Sea of Decay exists, if an area is surrounded by a black forest as overpowering as the sea, the term Sea of Decay would fit well. I wavered until the end over using the character for world instead of sea, but I decided on sea, thinking either would work fine. I took this term from the area called Sivash which actually exists in Crimea.

Takahashi: The image of the Sea of Decay is overwhelming. It was a shock to me to have the vague image we have of nature overturned. I wondered what brought about your depiction of nature with this evil image.

Miyazaki: At the end of Macbeth there is a scene of the forest moving. Macbeth has been told that as long as the forest doesn’t move, he will prevail, but in the end the forest moves. When I first heard the phrase “the forest moves” as a child, it sent shivers down my spine. I have long thought, “It’s all right if the forest moves. It’s all right to make a film like that.” I thought, “If trees had the power to curse people, human beings would have been killed off by their curses a long time ago.” With that idea in mind, I thought it would be interesting to overturn the concept of defenseless plants always being destroyed and instead create a forest that was on the offensive. In other words, isn’t it the height of arrogance to keep showing nature as needing protection to keep from disappearing? This is what I don’t care for. Everyone depicts nature as being charming. But it is something more fearsome. That is why I think there is something missing in our current view of nature. Even now, when we are in a forest, it is silent and quite frightening. There are paths that feel scary to all who walk them. Near the mountain cabin I often go to there is a road that feels creepy when I walk along it. I have asked the locals, “Did something happen on that road?” They answer, “No, that place just feels scary.” I agree with the slogan, “Nature is wonderful, nature is precious, we must protect nature,” but I want to think that it is only one part of the world we have. For the time being this is the case, but I feel we cannot forget that within our historic memory there was a period when the forest was overwhelmingly powerful. This is why a forest on the attack can exist.

Yamamoto: Deep in the Sea of Decay is a pure place that doesn’t require a breathing mask. And the old lady says, “We shouldn’t intentionally stop the encroachment of the Sea of Decay; it’s best to leave it to what will be.” Industrialized society is convinced that if it can subjugate nature it can win. What prompted you to think that this was not right and perceive that there is something pure in the depths of that which is polluted?

Miyazaki: When we try to live in that kind of world, that is the conclusion we are forced to reach. C. W. Nichol70 saw the film version and was very moved. When he asked me, “At the end there were buds sprouting; are you going to send a group of pioneers there?” I thought, “This fellow hasn’t changed at all. He’s just going to repeat the same thing.” I wanted to explain that my point of view was different, but it was too much of a bother, so I didn’t. What I did wasn’t in any way logical or articulate, but I did it because it matched the way I felt. I didn’t start drawing the story having composed its world, didn’t think from the start that the Sea of Decay was pure at its core.

Takahashi: That scene is very memorable for Nausicaä as well. She lies down and says, “Now, for the first time I understand the meaning of the Sea of Decay.” That scene made a deep impression on me.

Miyazaki: I realized after I drew the manga that I had been drawing something truly fantastic. While I was drawing the manga, I usually didn’t think about it much because I was just going with the flow of the story. Realizing what I had done later, I made the scene with the Sea of Decay a key element in the film, but while drawing the manga it was no more than if I had made a cast-off comment like, “What a beautiful sky.” In other words, I was dealing with material that would have collapsed if I had treated it in a logical way, so I had to go along with my feelings. I worked on it thinking, “This is more convincing to me,” and then, afterward, thinking about what I had done, I would try and justify it in the context of the story. This happened quite often.

Yamamoto: I think, to the contrary, that the scene shows a strong sense of logic. An alternate value is set up and a world harmonious to it naturally appears, out of necessity. The European world pushes off and eliminates oppositional existence, but Miyazaki-san, your Asian logic sets up a structure that moves us. You say it’s all accidental, but the very fact that this “event” is created by intuitive logic and the possibilities it creates are interesting. In addition, at first Nausicaä rides on her mehve using an engin, but later she rides the wind. While first she used heteronomous energy, later she uses nature’s autonomous energy, the wind. To my way of thinking, this is a logical metaphor for moving beyond industrial society, making it very interesting for me. I also thought that the image of machinery, or technology, that Miyazaki-san created in Nausicaä, was very interesting.

The image of technology

Takahashi: I also like Nausicaä, but my favorite is Castle in the Sky. I think the images of machinery in it are especially excellent. I have a very simple concern: is the world of Castle in the Sky the past or is it the near future? This is a real puzzle to me.

Miyazaki: I made it seem like science fiction written a long time ago. I wrote it as science fiction written in the days of steam engines. That is why I didn’t think trees would wither outside the earth’s atmosphere; in those days they wouldn’t have considered that trees would wither there, either. So I went full force with the last scene, thinking, “This is all right.” It’s best to think of it as a work of science fiction written at the end of the nineteenth century.

Yamamoto: Huge robots protected small flowers and eggs in Laputa.

Miyazaki: My view of machinery is a complete illusion. As I ride in my run-down car under the hot sun, I think, “Hell, it’s hot.” All the while I’m amazed: “In this heat the engine still works so well. How can it be so self-sacrificing, so devoted?” Humans make machines as extensions of their own hands, but at the same time we make something that will give us unlimited devotion. It’s too simplistic to consider them living beings, but I feel that we are making things that could be prototypes for living creatures. This means what is most noble in people’s hearts—devotion, self-sacrifice, things that aren’t so popular these days—is extremely simple. These machines are not born from something complex, but rather they consist of something much closer to the prototype, like a stone or other object has in this world. Music is the same. People have been giving form to what exists in space. People have merely turned into music the radio waves and vibrations that the stars and wind possess and continue to emit even when there is no one to hear them.

When I write this sort of thing, I think, “Music exists.” I don’t like to think that machinery keeps developing in one direction, endlessly becoming more complex. If I consider that machines can regress or turn back, then it might be possible for a spirit to dwell in machines. I like the guy who keeps riding an old motorcycle as he fixes it up because he relates to his machine with deep affection. I dislike someone who flips through a catalogue, says, “This is cool,” and buys a new one to replace the old. Isn’t he missing something important? I don’t like guys who trade in their cars every two years for a new one. I prefer people who detect a kind of animistic power in the marvel of machines.

Yamamoto: And Laputa is in the shape of a ship. It is shaped like a ship but flies through the air.

Miyazaki: There were many things like that in the nineteenth century. Jules Verne created the most interesting science fiction related to the undersea world. There have been many books written after his, now that we have more knowledge about diving into the sea, but they are all boring. Jules Verne’s story of going under the sea overlaps with entering into one’s own internal world. The fascination with the sea the story depicts and the idea that the sea hints at a far deeper and richer world full of secrets—this is the wonder Captain Nemo feels. At the same time, the sea shows the depth of his mind and the depth of the entire world itself.

What I find interesting is that when people imagine flying in the sky, they usually don’t think of flying in actual paragliders or being hoisted up by light cranes, because these things don’t fulfill their psychological yearnings. Instead, over and over again, they seem to imagine something else. This is why even when we are actually flying in the sky, our wish to fly in the sky under our own power exists as a separate desire. It’s a fascinating aspect of people’s minds.

Further, I think the time will come when we won’t be able to fix our air conditioners, there will be no electricity delivered, and we’ll be saying, “This is what we used to call a television and it showed video images.” It takes two weeks for an air conditioner to be repaired now, and when these things accumulate, I’m sure a time will come when we must face the fact that even if things worked in the past nothing works now. A time will come when maintenance isn’t kept up, so that power lines exist but electricity won’t be delivered. I dislike it when people insist things will always keep progressing in the same direction that we’re moving in now.

Yamamoto: That robot turned into a living kyoshinhei, or giant god-soldier, didn’t he?

Miyazaki: I struggled with that. Nausicaä was troubled as to what she could do, and so was I. That was a true act of the greatest devotion. It was most self-sacrificing, noble, and pure—the kyoshinhei have the greatest destructive power, but that was what they were made for. Even if we divide them into living and nonliving beings, no matter how devoted they are, you still cannot just treat them any way you like. That’s the same logic as not giving a damn about killing and eating a chicken because that is why you raised it. I don’t agree with that. I couldn’t help but think, “What if that machine or creature really started to speak words?” Then it becomes a problem, doesn’t it? Because then it means that we have to question ourselves about everything. So I didn’t draw the story with a full understanding of what I was doing. I got into more and more trouble as I went along. But I had to end it, so I did.

Yamamoto: Kamuiden (The Legend of Kamui) didn’t seem to end easily, either.

Miyazaki: No, it didn’t. Sanpei Shirato-san reached the point where his materialistic view of history became totally discredited. I’ve heard that there is a Legend of Kamui 2, but I find it hard to believe. There is no way it could continue. The world depicted in that story is untrue. When I read it, I thought, “History as class struggle is a lie, after all.” In the real world things are a bit more complex, ambivalent—even if there is a hierarchical class structure, it is full of cracks. That’s why people have been able to continue to survive.

Yamamoto: What moved me about The Legend of Kamui was the depiction of the people living in the cracks that appear in the principles of materialistic history. The nihilism of the dropout ninja Sanpei Shirato describes doesn’t fit into the historical materialist view, so he was forced to write a side-story “history” in Kamui gaiden.

Takahashi: Listening to you talk today, Miyazaki-san, I wonder if my way of looking at Castle in the Sky was wrong. I had thought Castle in the Sky’s main themes were honor, bravery, and devotion, and that these came from the main characters Pazu and Sheeta.

Miyazaki: Well, I think that honor, bravery, and devotion are very important in relations between people. But I’m sure that these qualities are not exclusive to human beings. When I made Castle in the Sky this wasn’t that clear to me. But now I think that these qualities are an essential element of this world, just as many of the adjectives we use were born from shapes in this world that predate humanity. What I wanted to say was, “Basically there is such a thing as Treasure Island. We don’t know what the treasure is, but it exists.” I also wanted to say that this treasure has a value different from the economic value implied in the comment, “If I find the treasure, how much will I get?”

Takahashi: I didn’t realize that, in the story, the image of devotion could have come from a thing like a robot.

Miyazaki: That’s fine with me. I didn’t make Castle in the Sky with that in mind. I just like things that way. But I ran into trouble because, overall, the island of Laputa had a negative image. In other words, I drew a type of diagram in which everything has a duality. In the end, while it is liberated from duality, Laputa is no longer Laputa, and it is released into the heavens. I drew the diagram in an arbitrary fashion because I don’t dislike the schema of drawing a duality and then at the end negating it. But in making a film based on such a schema I would have run the risk of the rug being pulled out from under my own schema.

Yamamoto: In my case, I think of the world of Castle in the Sky as the ultimate stage of industrial, technological society; and unable to come down to earth, it drifts off into the skies. When it goes, the machinery breaks down and only the trees are left. In contrast, in Nausicaä’s case, I think the idea of struggling to survive on a decaying earth becomes increasingly powerful.

Miyazaki: That’s the difference between film and manga. A film lasts only about two hours, limiting us considerably as to how much we can say. We can’t deal with a major theme. I usually feel sick when I read my own manga. When I’m feeling particularly well, I have flashes of thinking it’s interesting; when I’m not in good spirits, I’m appalled at what I’ve drawn … I have a bad habit of drawing manga that are hard to read. That’s my contrarian quality coming out. For me, drawing manga isn’t just an occupation, so I was determined to make it hard to read. When I started, I wanted to draw a manga that would be impossible for someone to read casually while slurping noodles. So I would become upset with myself whenever I felt it was becoming too easy to read. I would notice that there were fewer panels on the page, or that there were only eight, and then draw eleven panels to make the work even more difficult to read. And when Nausicaä came out in paperback books, I did silly things like adding more panels and even pages.

Yamamoto: I first read the manga and then saw the animation. The animation stands alone as a complete as a story, but there’s a certain chaotic quality in the manga that is a lot of fun.

Miyazaki: A film has to have an ending. There are those who claim a film can be anything the director wants, or a film should be like this or that. But if you show a paying audience something that seems to say, “The rest is up to you to think about,” you’re liable to hear people cry, “I’ve paid my money to have you think about it. So you come up with an answer.” If I were in the audience, I know I’d make the same demand.

Even if a film like Nausicaä is criticized for ending like a religious movie or a miracle movie, it still has to conform to the basics of filmmaking and it must follow a logical development. I’m one of those who feel that a film should show some problem being overcome, even if it’s a small one. I think, as filmmakers, that it’s a type of business ethic we have to maintain. But for manga stories, this sort of business ethic doesn’t apply. So the manga version of Nausicaä was a kind of penance for me. I stopped drawing the manga four times, thinking, “I’m done with this; I don’t want to draw it anymore.” But whenever I finished a film, the publishers would aske me to draw some more of the manga. And I would feel like I could. After about six months I would tell myself, “Okay, I’ll draw twenty-four pages a month; at times thirty-six pages.” But I would end up drawing sixteen pages, or at times only eight. My productivity was so low. I was battling the knots in my shoulders. When I read other people’s manga, I’m so impressed at how good they are. It’s true.

Critique of industrial society

Yamamoto: There are many discussions on reevaluating industrial society from philosophical and sociological perspectives. Similar debates arise from gekiga and animation, with the resultant formulation of a discourse taken to extremes. Yet I find that the Miyazaki world is far more symbolic and potent.

Miyazaki: Well, I don’t know about that. What each of us is asked is difficult to fulfill. It may be all right for us to discuss human society or ideological developments from a bird’s-eye view. But when we try to do this at the level of our daily lives, it doesn’t work so well. Still, we’ve come to the point where we should be having these discussions at the level of our daily lives. This is the greatest issue of our time. That’s why, when I started drawing Nausicaä, I was able to enjoy doing it and debating what sort of conclusion it should have. I wanted an aesthetic conclusion. And I’m sure it was the same for Ōtomo when he was creating Akira. Then I entered a period where I realized that human beings would continue to increase in number and continue with their messy and listless ways. One might ask, “Are we just left with religion, then?” but I don’t think religion will come as a solution all of a sudden. These days I am acutely curious as to what kind of faces Christ and Shakyamuni had. I’ve come to the conclusion that they couldn’t have been very handsome. Rather, they must have had faces that would make others want to crucify them.

Yamamoto: In issue no. 33 of this magazine, Takeshi Umehara-san71 states that the world’s four greatest spiritual men, including Shakyamuni, Christ, and Confucius, all created human-centered doctrines, and that we must go beyond this way of thinking. The crux of this, he said, is the forest and the philosophy of the forest. Agriculture, particularly wheat growing, has destroyed the forests. Rice-growing agriculture left the forests relatively safe and has been somehow able to maintain a balance with water resources, but it nonetheless has problems of its own, according to Umehara.

Miyazaki: I understand that, but rice-growing areas have high population densities. If we could go back to the stage you’re talking about it would be all well and good. In effect, though, agriculture itself was the result of humans facing dire straits. Human beings haven’t become wealthy from cultivating crops; they have only become able to increase to greater numbers while avoiding starvation. That is the reality of humans. The book A Green History of the World: The Environment and the Collapse of Great Civilizations by Clive Ponting, deals with this, and I think the birth of this way of thinking indicates a major shift. In terms of considering the history of living beings as part of the earth’s history, NHK’s special programs Chikyū dai kikō (Great Journeys on Earth) and Seimei (Life) have had a great impact on mass audiences. We learned from these programs that when oxygen became part of the atmosphere, it was a deadly poison to living beings on earth. We also learned of the results of research on the Burgess Shale Formation and the Copernican revolution. People say that the Cambrian age explosion was a coincidence; that our current form and the fact that we are still here are also coincidental. But if this is to become the basis of our way of thinking as we continue living on earth, what types of thought will emerge from that? The commonly accepted evolutionary theory of the survival of the fittest has had a significant effect on us. But if there are problems with the theory, what would the result be? It could mean great changes for the twenty-first century. It might turn out that dinosaurs didn’t become extinct because they became so large.

Takahashi: Living creatures appear suddenly, and then disappear suddenly, for no obvious reason. This has been repeated many times in the history of living things. The idea that living things have evolved in a linear progression toward a set goal has been discredited. This was shown in the clearest way in the NHK special on Anomalocaris, an extinct Cambrian “anomalous shrimp” (NHK Science Special, Seimei: Yonjūokunen harukana tabi 2 (Life: The Long Journey of 4 Billion Years 2).

Miyazaki: The scale models they used were amazing.

Takahashi: I thought that was so Japanese. I don’t think the British would have thought of using models like that.

Miyazaki: For a while the words Hallucigenia and Opabinia were even popular at our studio. I had a big laugh when I saw that Hallucigenia was pictured upside down in the book Wonderful Life by Stephen Jay Gould.

I don’t know what way of thinking will control our response to these issues. Even if the environment is at the mercy of the usual, dull thinking of democracy—which we are forced to deal with—I still think we’re facing an incredibly major change. We’re facing an age when the world’s population will be ten billion. And how are we to relate this to the concrete issues of how we live our daily lives, and what we are to do about old age pressing down on us? We lack that connection between the vast changes we face and our immediate concerns. I don’t want to be considered an ecologist, so I puff away on my cigarettes. We think cel animation is better than computer graphics, yet at the same time we face the problem of industrial waste, so we ask whether it makes sense to continue producing films with cels. But we end up leaving things ambivalent.

Yamamoto: I think there is nothing as artificial as the idea of preserving nature. What you depict in your films is a world that transcends this way of thinking.

Miyazaki: Near my house in Tokorozawa there is a dirty river that has been cleaned up a bit since sewers were built. It was a clean stream forty years ago; at its worst it turned milky white and nothing could possibly live there. Then it became a bit cleaner and became a habitat for nonbiting midges. There were so many of these bluish insects that we could have cooked them up as a dish with soy sauce and sugar. Even though their appearance is usually a sign of environmental decay, in this location it was proof that the river had revived somewhat. It had been a place where even midges couldn’t survive. For ten years or so there were outbreaks of these nonbiting midges. Then, when the river became even cleaner, the outbreaks stopped. A program for citizens to clean up the river began and I participated in it. A middle-aged woman who participated just once said that it allowed her to physically understand the kind of difficult ideas that eminent scholars write about. She and other participants understood that a specific type of conservation of the river bank wouldn’t work, that it wouldn’t make the river clean. But then when some sand accumulated, the public works office would smooth it over with a bulldozer; and the riverbed that had stabilized for a habitat for fish would be completely leveled. The participants in the program were angered by this.

There was no question of being leftist or rightist in this movement. When I participated, I felt more pleased by seeing a real crayfish than by some grandiose feeling that I was preserving nature. We were able to become simply happy rather than thinking we were providing aid to protect this or that. We could see that the river was getting closer to what we remembered as children. The last time I went there, an old man was sitting in the river, bathing, and I wondered, “What’s he doing when the water isn’t that clean yet?” That’s about the only thing I’m doing in particular, and it’s only about four times a year, but I think my neighborhood river is better if it’s clean rather than dirty and foul smelling. If we do these sorts of things in our own limited way, thinking how wonderful it would be if we could go fishing here, in just an hour of crawling around in the riverbed we can understand what it would take a renowned scholar to tell us in a two-hour lecture. About fifteen of us worked to pull out a motorbike that was buried in the riverbed, cheering each other on, shouting “Wasshoi, wasshoi!” so heartily that those who lived nearby probably thought there was a festival going on. I discovered that I love this feeling of satisfaction. I figure that, for the time being, this is fine to have as a routine activity. We tout “protecting nature” as our aim, but the river itself doesn’t care whether it has a motorbike buried in it or not. Creating a cleaner river is merely our goal, so it is no doubt meaningless in terms of the overall environmental index. We were excited to see fish again, but those fish are no doubt suffering from asthma or atopic dermatitis.

Even so, let’s give the program credit for now. In the past I didn’t greet people I saw on the street, but now when I see members of the river-cleaning group we acknowledge each other. At a very basic level, I feel more at ease in my surroundings, and think, hey, I’ve come to belong here. I have no idea how this relates to difficult environmental issues, but I’ve decided to live this way on a daily basis. I won’t become one of the leaders of such a movement, but at this level it’s not about the politics of the Liberal Democratic Party. Talking with the LDP’s city assemblyman while picking up empty cans and putting them into bags, we did manage to agree in a very simple way that it’s better that the river is clean. He was probably thinking, “It’s good if this also helps me in the next election.” And recently, I’ve come to think that’s fine with me.

Yamamoto: Why do you always have girls as your main characters?

Miyazaki: It’s not from any logical thinking. It just seems to me that when you compare a boy doing something with a girl doing that same thing, the girl is much more spirited. If a boy is walking with long strides I think nothing of it, but if a girl is walking boldly, I think she looks so full of vitality. That’s because I’m a man; women might think a boy striding along looks cool. At first I thought, “It’s no longer a man’s world; no longer an age when just causes are relevant.” But after ten years, it seems silly to keep saying that. So now, when people ask me your question, I just respond, “Because I like girls.” That answer seems more real.

Takahashi: You focus on girls, but listening to you talk, you also give a lot of attention to children in general.

Miyazaki: With actual children I’m the type who hits it off with those I really like but can’t connect with those I don’t hit it off with right away. My friends’ children come by, and those I get along with right from the start I become friends with. Other times it’s hopeless, as when a baby bawls upon meeting me. So I’ve stopped thinking that all children are cute. There are those I just don’t get along with. I tell my friends, “Unfortunately, it looks like I just can’t get along with your kid.” This may be a cliché, but I think young children can get used to anything. They gradually become more boring. Three-year-olds are more fun than five-year-olds. When you compare five-year-olds and schoolchildren, the older ones become more complex but in contrast they become easier to understand. What the world has given them is swapped for what people give them. I used to think I wanted grandchildren, but nowadays I don’t really care. I think it’s fine as long as I can relate to young children all the time. After all, children pass through that stage and keep growing. Our work remains the same; children keep passing through, but there are always new children to encounter. That, at least, is how I have come to think of things.

Yamamoto: Nausicaä takes the passive stance of initially not wanting to do anything, doesn’t she? If it were a boy, it seems to me that he would have done something, but a girl wouldn’t have. And yet Nausicaä is eventually forced to join in the struggle.

Miyazaki: I hadn’t realized that. I only became aware of that as I worked on the story … I thought initially I was drawing the story without knowing what she was trying to do. She wanted to avoid the tragedy that she saw in front of her, and she initially only tried to alleviate the symptoms in a haphazard manner. She doesn’t think she can prevent the core problems. She takes action to try to see what is about to happen. So she does stop being passive. It was only much later that I became conscious of this.

Making movies for children

Takahashi: I mentioned the existence of a pure world at the bottom of the Sea of Decay. I don’t know if this relates, but Nausicaä raises plants from the Sea of Decay in her own room. I thought that the adults in the story would probably never have come up with this concept. I thought that the existence of a pure world at the bottom of the Sea of Decay was paired up with raising those plants in the human world. To me it seems that we can see the meaning of Nausicaä by inverting the concepts of clean and dirty, good and evil.

Miyazaki: As for me, that didn’t occur to me until I got to that panel. I hadn’t thought at the start what the inside of the castle, with Nausicaä’s secret room, would even look like. When I was wondering, “Where would Nausicaä be at this particular point in time?” I found she was in that room. I only realized this afterward. I added my own sense of logic to the story later, by questioning whether something was really possible, or whether specific characters might really have existed. It’s a lot like making a film. You can make a film up to a certain point using logic. In the preproduction planning stage you think in a logical manner that if we do it this way, we can make it. Or you put it together thinking, if we do such and such we can bring it to an end.

However, when you enter into the actual production process, it all becomes unfathomable and you feel like you can’t proceed. You can no longer rely on the part that was made with logic, the part I call made by the cerebral cortex. It becomes useless. The film can’t be completed unless you think with your subconscious. That is why you must become desperate. When you are faced with real hardship, convinced that your plans are no good, your subconscious mind thinks for you and lo and behold an answer comes. When the answer comes in that form, I really feel like I’m creating a film. That’s when I know I have the right answer.

I think my films are not inside my head, but are in a space above my head. The films already exist. It sounds impressive when I say I’m being creative, but that’s not what’s really going on. There is only a single best solution given the combination of my present abilities and the objective conditions in which I am placed. Once I decide on a method for the production and a direction, although there are many ways of determining the direction, there is only one way to proceed each time. My work consists of nothing more than discovering how I can get as close to that direction as possible. The film tries to become a film. The filmmaker just becomes a slave to the film. The relationship is not one of me creating the film, but rather of the film forcing me to create it.

Yamamoto: A narrative that emphasizes causality and continuity lets us see the next steps in the plot but doesn’t necessarily move us emotionally. But with Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind, even when there are discontinuities in the story line and we are pointed to things whose presence is not resolved, we are nonetheless moved. We are moved by a world of discontinuity that causes us to make great leaps in thought.

Miyazaki: Our brains seem at first glance to be logical, but the structure of our brains is not. That is why I believe that the most important thing is our intuition. When we’re asleep this is what does our thinking for us. There may be a certain way to do things according to logic, but as filmmakers we can take things anyway we want. There is nothing that says that we have to do things in a specific way. We can choose anything we want. With freedom to choose anything, we just have to choose what we think is best. Working as a film director I recognize the truth of the old Japanese adage: “Logic and plaster salve can stick to anything.” We can eloquently argue why we should do it this or that way. Then, we decide that we’re convinced we should do it a particular way. Yet we might just as well argue for a different method, saying that it would be better after all.

Yamamoto: What made you come up with Totoro?

Miyazaki: I didn’t want Totoro to be a bear or a tanuki raccoon dog. But this was really just a vague idea. While I was making the film, I couldn’t figure it out, so I became anxious. What I was sure about was that the creature’s face should be inscrutable; it should be hard to know what it was thinking. I wasn’t able to understand the effect of this when I was making the film and I didn’t know what I had done until at least six months after the film was completed. I had no idea that the audience would react as it did.

Takahashi: My daughter was four years old when the film came out and her whole nursery school went to see the movie. I was free that day so I went along as a parent volunteer to help the teachers deal with fifty children. They were ages three to six, and I could tell that the children were really responding to the scenes as the film progressed. When the cat bus appeared, they yelled in excitement. The children’s response was so vivid I was surprised. And I was swept along by their excitement.

Miyazaki: When two or three young children enjoy something, the adults around them become happy, too. This is really true. The only time I saw the film at a movie theater was for the test screening. There were a few young children there, and when they were happy the adults near them became happy. That kind of emotion is contagious.

Takahashi: That’s true. That kind of feeling was clearly communicated to us as well.

Miyazaki: When that happens, I’m happy, too. I just think, “I’m glad.” I was delighted to hear that when the film was shown at a kindergarten, the children hid under their chairs when they saw Mei going into Totoro’s cave. For children, what is scary and eerie is mixed with what is cute and fun. Feeling excitement is a bit scary. I think it’s a mistake to separate out what is cute, like little birds, flowers, and butterflies, from what is creepy or what causes damage. The worlds of beer bottles, cider bottles, and butterflies are all connected in some way. When we separate things according to an adult value system and suggest to children that, for example, when they see a snake they should scream in fright, we end up creating very boring children. Although we expound on difficult subjects, as living beings we are made so that when young children are really enjoying something nearby—not just movies, but something in daily life, and not just our own children—we become happy as well. This is so true that I’ve decided to always try to honestly recognize these feelings.

Takahashi: I realized what you’ve just explained for the first time when I watched your films. I think there is a strong connection between what I felt raising my children and what I feel watching your films. And this relates to what I wanted to ask about children. In having children, for the first time I felt empathy for a developing life and for the image of life and nature that empathy allowed me to have. This is why I think I relate Miyazaki-san’s films to my own childrearing experiences and continue to feel a strong sense of realism in them.

Miyazaki: I am left only with an irreparable regret about my own children. I was always working and away from home during the time they were the most fun. On occasions when I was home I would be excessively generous; it wasn’t a normal, daily-life relationship at all. Sure, I took them to Toshimaen amusement park and told them, “Go ahead, go on all the rides.” But I was just spoiling them; and I feel I missed out on many things I can’t revisit. That is why I now feel it doesn’t matter whose child it is, I just want to share the moment. Just that alone can bring pleasure. It’s not easy to keep associating with children over the course of a long time. Raising children is such an effort, and only when it’s over do we realize, “Next time, I’ll be able to do it a bit better.” But by that time you’ve exhausted your physical energy. It is so true that parents are also being raised as they raise their children.

Being allowed to make films for young children is a wonderful gift that other generations haven’t been able to experience. This is priceless to me. I doubt that I would have wanted to make films for little children if I hadn’t become a parent. Most of all we want to make films to satisfy ourselves. There are many among our staff who are thirty years old but haven’t gotten married. But if you have a child and it turns three or so, you feel, “I have to show this kid something good.” And then you think, “There’s nothing good out there, so I’ll make a film myself.” We think we’ve gotten to this stage by doing that. Buffeted as we have been by the upheavals of the world in the past few years, however, both Isao Takahata-san and I think we have also ended up making films that seemed to be merely ways to justify ourselves. Nonetheless, we continue to tell ourselves that we need to return to our starting point and make more movies for children.
