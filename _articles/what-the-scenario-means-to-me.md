Kōza Animation 3 ‘Image Design’: Bijutsu Shuppansha: January 15, 1989

Introduction

My experience with animation is limited to the genres in which I have worked: several television series and theatrical release films. I have never studied scriptwriting, nor have I made any effort to read the scenarios of projects other than my own. In the worst cases, I have been known to just skim through the scenarios of projects that I am involved with and leave the rest to intuition and feelings.

For me to theorize about scenarios, I can only rely on my biased viewpoint and use my experience to show the hole I have dug to fit my own case. Rather belatedly, I should point out that I am probably not the best person to discuss this subject. For the scenario writer, it may be that he must perfect a scenario in order to present it as his own work. However, the real work is the film, and the tasks that go into finishing the completed film are all parts of the process. To put it bluntly, as long as the film is made, it doesn’t matter what method was used to create the scenario.

I think there is no set method or procedure for making a film. Those involved in the making of a film make a concerted effort, step by step, to create the film they want to make; the entire process is the creation of the film.

The current division of labor among the staff’s job categories—animators who direct the drawings and artists who create the key animation drawings, the director or creative director or overall director, and the supervisor—is no more than what is specific to a certain time and a certain place. Production procedures such as planning, scenario writing, storyboarding, and setting the characters and stage are categories born merely of the need for production efficiency. If the situation changes, a different procedure will emerge. All tasks can be done at the same time if that would be better. There are countless cases in which the scenario is enriched because the writer is inspired by the drawings, or the artists’ imaginations are enriched by the scenario. There are examples of scenarios being written just to make the story coherent after a storyboard was created, and I’m sure I can’t be the only one who has finished a film without a scenario.

It is the height of folly to think that one’s work is more than just one part of the complicated process of making a film. If a change suggested by another person improves the work, it is a mistake to refuse to incorporate it under the misconception that one’s purview has been violated. Animation is a collaborative process. Writers who believe that refusing to make scenario changes protects their work and directors who use their artistic rights as a shield to prevent changes in the storyboard simply prove that they are not talented at what they do. What is needed when involved in collaborative work is the flexibility to use without hesitation a better plan than one’s own (even if it comes from a rookie hired the day before) and the determination to convince others through discussion to adopt the ideas one believes in.

Here I digress to state that there are those, even now, who consider that the Disney Productions method of animation production is the best. In the Disney method, a group of main staff works on preparation, creates an image board, comes up with a story, and develops a storyboard. Then everyone suggests ideas. The members of the group are reassembled and shuffled; the storyboard is made into a film for further discussion (called a “Leica reel”5). They take a sample of the movements, and then complete the preparation process. In Japan, people tend to idealize the Disney method because it seems to draw on the creativity of everyone involved. Unfortunately this is a falsehood. This method, created by Walt Disney and his close associates, was the method best suited to their time, their film content (or goals, perhaps), and those people who were then gathered together. And in order to maintain this system, Disney Productions let go of talented employees who did not fit in with their method. I think films should be created according to their times and places (by “place” I mean national conditions, including cultural differences), using different methods and taking into account the talent, enthusiasm, and stamina of the staff assembled. Some in Japan believed that following the methodology of a past golden age—probably a past when scarcity added value to theatrical showings of animation and audiences wanted more—would give birth to excellent films. Several examples of using the Disney-style storyboard method have been attempted in Japan. I stand by my assertion that they all have failed.

A system must adjust to the people involved. When we look into what is considered a good system, we find that it is one supported by personnel with talent. When even one talented person pulls out, a good system can turn ineffective and bureaucratic. It is best to create, each time, the method that is best suited to the kind of work you want to create and the people you can assemble.

Going back to scenarios, I think the role of the scenario should be determined by the needs of the production. Don’t think that I am saying that technique and ability are not important. Without technique and effort, even if you dream of a wonderful work, you cannot make a decent film no matter how much money you pour into the project. In order to polish your technique, you need to accumulate experience, as well as have talent and enthusiasm.

What is a scenario?

Scenarios were not initially created for use in animation production. They were adapted from stage plays for use in live-action films. When animation films evolved from early works with simple content to become complex and long, people started creating scenarios for animation as well.

Instead of attempting to define what an animation scenario is (something I feel incapable of doing), I will note my thoughts by giving a few hypothetical examples. Let us say I am unexpectedly handed a scenario. As I read through the script, my imagination is stimulated, I become excited, and I am so moved that I would love to turn it into a film. If this script were used to make a film it would be the foundation, the starting point of everything. The scenario would become the central force that gathered the people, drew out the talents of the staff, excited their creativity, and pulled together everyone’s strengths and skills. Even if the scenario has not attained perfection, if I felt certain that the rough stone could be turned into a gem, I would want to polish it and let it shine. I have not come across such a case, but I think that this can happen for stage plays and live-action films.

When people think of scenarios, they think of material that has this kind of power. In such a case, the scenario embodies in written text what the creator wants to express and with an overall clarity that can carry the plan for the creation of the film, the particulars, the flow of time, the words to be spoken, and the characters themselves.

What do you do when you have to make a film based on an imperfect scenario—one that is unconvincing or even seems unusable?

Normally, you should work in collaboration with the screenwriter to rework the scenario, but often that is out of the question. Usually a writer has determined that his scenario is satisfactory, or that any further revisions are not worth the pay. In most cases the administrative work of accepting the scenario as the final version has already been completed and there is no time for rewrites. This situation is rampant in the current Japanese animation world.

We can only use this kind of scenario as a draft. Though the writer’s name will be in the credits, the only thing we can do is to take it apart entirely and do the work of putting in different content. Even if we don’t go that far, we are dogged by the need for small and medium revisions.

In this case, a confrontation normally develops between the director and the writer and his supporters (the person in charge of literary arts, at times the producer). As there is no possibility of compromise, the alternatives are either to quit or to make the other quit—or to obfuscate the scenario in some way. This develops into a power struggle that can’t be helped.

However, if you don’t have an alternative plan, this approach is impossible to take. When you criticize another person’s work at the workplace, you need to have an alternative plan and the ability to persuade others that your option is better. The workplace has no use for a critic. If you don’t have this persuasive ability, you have to grit your teeth and work with the original scenario until you develop an assertive voice. This is a hard thing to do.

I have drifted from discussing the scenario to the actual work process. What I want to get across is that I have not gone about my work thinking of myself as filling a certain job category such as scenario writer, director, or art director. The content of any job title will be determined by the power relationships among the people assembled to work on the project. A staff member who is an art designer may be powerful enough to have a voice in the directing and drawing areas; a powerless director may be influenced by a powerful animator. These are normal occurrences in this industry.

This is what I learned from my seniors when I was starting out and my own experience has confirmed it. When I discuss scenarios as a filmmaker, I tend to take this viewpoint. If I were asked to discuss directing, I would no doubt write about the same thing. As I noted earlier, this discussion is the product of my biased viewpoint and my actual experience.

When the project has finally been approved, whether based on a story or an original script, how does the filmmaking start? When there is an original manga, the basic image and story already exist, so it might seem easy, but that is not the case. Story manga have too many story lines, and it takes all one’s energy just to sort them out. When we work on a film, we have to start by tearing everything apart. Whether the original is a literary work or a manga, the workload is the same for those who turn it into an animated film.

First, we must build an organic whole consisting of the approach to the entire film: the strategic goal (what we want to say), the tactical goal (what we want to achieve), the period setting, location setting, and characters to fulfill this story. Many people start by focusing on writing a scenario, but in the case of animation, I think we work more efficiently without doing this. Even without spending time on writing a scenario, we always lack preparation time. I think it is more effective to use this limited time to create an image board. That said, drawing and writing should proceed simultaneously, and notes should be taken on their progress.

The order sent out to the writer is based on these notes and drawings. Understand that the first draft of any script will never be satisfactory. One way to use the preparation time—that is the time before the entire staff starts on the project—is to continually revise the scenario until the last moment. Or, if there is no time for this, the director and the main staff need to comprehend the entire concept of the work through the scenario that is in hand—if this is an easier way to have everyone grasp the idea, it may be the only way to go.

I myself stop working on the scenario with the first version. That is because I never have enough preparation time, and I have always worked under conditions where it seems impossible to realize a final scenario even after I request one. This may show my poor man’s mentality, but in fact in this industry we exist in a state of poverty.

Fully aware that it is not finalized, we print the scenario when we see the large outline of the work. This scenario, in book form with a cover, is not for sponsors or the production company, but for us to take an objective look at all the work done up to that time.

With this as a starting point—and if necessary we pull the scenario apart, chop it up and change it, and we’re even willing to throw it all out—we work up a more exacting plan. My opinion is that the preparation work is finalized with the completion of the storyboard, so all my energies are aimed toward this end.

In addition to serving as a starting point, the printed scenario allows us to calculate the scope of the entire project. This is a very important role, as at times—indeed, usually—we are forced to start drawing from the completed segments before the storyboard is finalized. The scenario is thus useful as a barometer to gauge our progress. If, for example, we are one-fourth of the way through the pages of the scenario, then the total number of seconds in the storyboard should be approximately one-fourth of the whole.

Theatrical films may have looser requirements than television programs, but that doesn’t mean we can arbitrarily set the number of feet for the film. If we begin the drawing process before the storyboard is completed and have a thirty-minute overrun on a less than two-hour long film, we will have wasted a large amount of work, the staff’s labor will not be compensated, the editing will be hellish, and the film will end up showing wounds from these cuts.

Readers may see these thoughts as slighting the screenwriter. Indeed, there are quite a few writers who oppose me. It may seem reasonable for them to be angry when—after have been given a detailed plot, handed pictures, told to read the notes, and write it up as suggested—their scenario is used only as a starting point and the resulting film is entirely different. But I think it is wrong for them to get angry. The director as well as the main staff should be throwing the ball back and forth with the writer. No matter how painstaking the work of writing the scenario, one toss of the ball is not enough. What is required is to recognize the intent of the entire work process. That includes your own trial and error efforts as a film is made, based ultimately on the mutual trust gained from throwing the ball, catching what is thrown back at you, and throwing the ball again.

At a certain point in the history of the film industry, the scenario writer was given special privileges. He gained citizenship for doing work that embodied creative writing and individuality. No doubt he fought for and gained that privileged position by writing good scripts.

But it is wrong to adapt this, as is, to animation. I am not demanding that the rights of the writer be limited. I am pointing out that other important staff members suffer from a severe lack of rights. It is a mistake to view the screenwriter and the director as being special as far as creating animated films is concerned. To give an example, it is unconscionable when too much emphasis is placed on the original work, with the director and screenwriter getting only trifling amounts from secondary-use fees of the film, and other staff members getting nothing. This problem encompasses many difficult issues that cannot easily be discussed and resolved. An organic way of working is hardly possible for a staff that is bound by rights and responsibilities set out in a thick stack of contract papers.

In essence, I think that the writer’s role in animation is not to fixate on a book that has his or her name on it, but to actively participate in the creation of the film as a member of the team. Only then does that film become something created by the entire staff—and at the same time something to which the writer has contributed that he or she can claim as his own work.

Actual scenario cases

My name is on several films as screenwriter. There are also several films that do not credit me as screenwriter although I performed that function. Leaving aside the issue of how those films were received or whether they are worthy of discussion, I will note here the actual situation concerning scenarios on a few of the films I have made.

▶ Panda! Go, Panda! (Panda kopanda);theatrical short: Tokyo Movie, A Pro: 1972

Told to come up with any kind of project starring a panda, Isao Takahata6 and I worked up this idea in one night or two. The decision to greenlight the production came a long time later, after a news announcement that a panda was arriving from China. Then the project was immediately given the go-ahead.

We talked about a simple idea and what kind of film we wanted to make and sent it out to a certain screenwriter. While waiting for the first version of the scenario, we created an image board in the style of a storyboard, with specifics fleshing out our idea.

Dissatisfied with the first version of the script, we decided it would take too long to wait for a second version; we received the company’s approval on the condition that we would write the scenario. Takahata and I talked the story over, and I wrote it up based on the image board.

The text wasn’t in the form of a scenario, so I asked the person in charge of continuity to write it up clearly so it could be printed, and then started working on the storyboard with Takahata.

In the case of this film, we were in clear accord in our aim, thanks to the work we had previously done together. As I had written the scenario with the intention of making it a basis for our work, it was a self-styled document with a far greater number of stage directions than usual. At the storyboard stage, the pictures I had drawn for the script were expertly arranged by Takahata. The features I subconsciously had in mind for the characters were transformed into the language of film. This was very satisfying to me. I have never had more pleasure as I worked.

For the sequel, Panda! Go, Panda! Rainy-Day Circus (Panda Kopanda: Amerfuri circus no maki), we decided from the start that I would write the scenario. Lounging around at Takahata’s house, he and I chatted about what kind of story it should be and discovered that we shared a childhood attraction to floods. I wrote up a scenario that was very much like production notes. As I recall, I again asked the continuity person to write it up clearly.

As this was not a story that began with a dramatic event and then unfolded (our aim was precisely to avoid such a structure), a writer trying to develop a scenario in the usual fashion would not have understood our intent. Indeed, when it was written up as a script, it seemed extremely ordinary, and it would have been natural for anyone to worry if the material could be turned into a film. Yet I still believe that what we were aiming for was correct.

▶ Future Boy Conan (Mirai Shōnen Konan); television series, Nippon Animation: 1978

I took on directing this project on the assumption that I could make major revisions to the original story. So I created a rough framework for the story, wrote the plot with as much detail as I could, and held discussions with the writer. It was then that I realized for the first time that when the plot was looked at objectively, as a scenario, it was full of holes. I was confronted with the problem that even though the scenario reflected my plot ideas, I was unable to make a storyboard out of it.

Every week, in my job as director, I would put in an order for the scenario and storyboard, but the result was that the plot, scenario, and the storyboard all became a basis for another reworking of the original scenario. Moreover, the story began to go off in a different direction than I had initially planned, so we had to create the storyboards anew. The schedule slipped and I caused a lot of trouble for the staff and the production company. This was entirely due to my failings, as I can only work following my intuition and feelings.

But whether someone else creates the scenario or I do it myself, I see the scenario as a basic starting point from which I can make changes. What is more, it is often from the process of negating something that we see the direction we should be moving in.

For example, even if it is only a note scribbled on a single sheet of writing paper, the storyboard can come together in a single flowing act of creation if it comes from an idea that has been well thought out. When you stumble over something or when the plot doesn’t seem quite clear, there is a problem hidden somewhere. But if you break down what you have piled up and look for another approach, you will eventually realize that there is only one way to go forward again. When the schedule is pressing, you want to get through somehow and you want to believe that all is going well, but it is important not to stifle even the smallest doubt. Only by being able to make the decision to trash a storyboard that you finished after several all-nighters can you see what portions can be used and what cannot be used.

What proves reliable is not logic but intuition. I do want to say that, to Takahata’s credit, the storyboards he drew for five of the episodes were of a high level of perfection, and in my exhausted state this was as much of a relief as rain during a drought.

▶ Lupin III: The Castle of Cagliostro (Rupan sansei: Kariosutoro no shiro); feature-length film: Tokyo Movie Shinsha, Telecom: 1979

My responsibilities for this film started with creating the story. In order to give form to my vague idea, I began by drawing a bird’s-eye-view picture of the setting for the story: the lake and castle of a small country. When I completed this drawing, I was confident that the film would go well. As to the scenario, we agreed that the credit would be shared and changes would be accepted at the storyboard stage. I created the story through to the last major scene and gave my treatment to the screenwriter. This first draft scenario was printed without change to its rough edges and with no divergence from the nuances of my ideas. At the same time, I wrote out the sequences on a large sheet of paper, put this up on the wall, and began working on the storyboard. This was my way of checking to see how much I had covered of the entire flow of sequences; as I drew portions onto the storyboard, I crossed the sequences out with a magic marker on the paper on the wall.

In a work like Lupin, the story line may be set, but the important thing is how it is depicted, how characters enter and depart, and the story’s hidden traps and subplots are portrayed. As such, plot changes are unavoidable when turning the story into a film.

I divided the plot turns and conclusion into four parts—A, B, C, and D—and as we proceeded in our tasks to part C, it became clear that we were considerably over the foot length and that we would end up way over the set film length. So we made midcourse modifications to simplify the plotline. As to part D, we determined that due to the tight schedule, we couldn’t complete all the tasks required for our initial intended content, and we made a strategic withdrawal at the storyboard stage.

Because of this compromise, I felt a sense of psychological defeat for half a year after completion of the film. Even so, I do not think my method, which may look irregular at first glance, was a mistake.

▶ Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind (Kaze no tani no Naushika); feature-length film, Top Craft: 1984

For this, I followed the same method as for The Castle of Cagliostro, but it took a long time for me to dismantle my own original story, and this placed me in dire straits. Despite having arranged for a cowriter for the scenario, I was unable to make effective use of this method. In the end, as time ran out, I had to start the film process with just the plot pasted up on the wall. My name is listed as the screenwriter, but for this work I didn’t even write a basic scenario; it was only to satisfy the common format in the credits that we listed my name and prepared a written scenario as if there had been one. My conclusion is that this wasn’t a mistake in method. However, I would not encourage anyone who has completed a story in one format (manga, in this case) to then try to render it into another format, in this case film.

* * * *

This method of producing the final version of the scenario after the film has been completed dates back to Animal Treasure Island (Dōbutsu takarajima) in 1971, when the main staff made that decision. It was the first time, in my experience, that we formally used a highly developed storyboard as the basis for making changes in the scenario. This method is used not just by me but also by other directors. Some continually fight with their writers while others have collaboration-minded writers as colleagues, ensuring a smooth process. While this method arises from the realities of the work process, I’m sure it prevents leaps of imagination beyond a certain point, as does the experiential method. Also, there is the danger of too much concentration of authority in the hands of the director and the potential for deterioration that comes from sticking to old habits.

As I noted earlier, we are always in search of scenarios that stun those working on the project and point to a new horizon. We must continue to be humble as there is always the potential of coming across a powerful scenario whose brilliance fulfills the original essence of what a scenario should be.

How to write the best scenario

I do not know how to write the best scenario. If I knew, I would be able to write one. Scenario-writing ability comes from talent and effort and is not something that can be taught. It is not a technique but emerges from what one wants to express.

One must have the clear core of what one wants to convey. This is the trunk of the story that penetrates throughout in a strong and simple way. What catches the audience’s eye is the treetop, the shimmer of the leaves. What is most required of a scenario are roots that spread deep into the earth and a strong trunk hidden by the mass of shimmering leaves. As long as there is a trunk strong enough to support branches and leaves, the rest—hanging decorations, letting flowers bloom, and adding accessories—can be accomplished by everyone sharing their ideas.

The best scenario might be one that includes the mass of leaves and even the insects that crawl among those leaves.
