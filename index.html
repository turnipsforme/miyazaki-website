Hayao Miyazaki

1941 — 2025

Eighty-four years of Hayao Miyazaki, year by year: from a wartime Tokyo childhood to two Academy Awards — every film, retirement, and return. Tōei Animation · Nippon Animation · Studio Ghibli.

Miyazaki Airplane · Gakushuin

1941–1963

Before animation: a wartime childhood among aeroplane parts, a long absence of his mother, and one film — seen at university — that set everything in motion.

1941aged 0

Born in wartime Tokyo

Hayao Miyazaki is born January 5 in Akebonobashi, Shinjuku, Tokyo, the second of four sons[1]. His father, Katsuji, manages “Miyazaki Airplane”, the family business run by Hayao’s uncle: a factory cutting rudder parts for the Mitsubishi A6M Zero fighter. Wartime evacuation takes the family to the countryside; two of his early memories are of an evacuated, half-deserted town and a mother too ill to embrace him.

Childhood · Miyazaki Airplane · Evacuation to Utsunomiya and Ōmiya

The factory shaped him permanently. He later traced his obsession with flight — the beauty of machines, and their terrible cargo — to those years surrounded by warplanes and by men who made them. The Zero returns at the centre of his final feature, The Wind Rises.

1947aged 6

Mother hospitalised

His mother, Yoshiko, falls ill with spinal tuberculosis and spends much of 1947–1955 in hospitals. Young Hayao rides on his father’s bicycle to visit her. Decades later he would pour this memory into My Neighbor Totoro: the bedridden mother Yasuko, the children who cannot reach her, the house in the countryside — the film is widely understood as semi-autobiographical, something he has never denied.

1958aged 17

Gakushuin · The Tale of the White Serpent

After studying political science and economics at Gakushuin University — where he also writes for the children’s literature research club — a classmate drags him to see Tōei’s first colour animated feature, The Tale of the White Serpent (released October 1958)[2]. He is transfixed. He has said the film convinced him animation could carry real feeling — the sorrows and joys of ordinary people — not just gags for children.

Miyazaki later recalled falling in love with animation that night — and, specifically, with the heroine’s passionate longing, which showed him animation could be about feeling rather than gags.

His recollection of The Tale of the White Serpent, as told in interviews collected in Starting Point: 1979–1996

1963aged 22First job · Animator

Joins Tōei Animation

Graduates from Gakushuin and joins Tōei Animation in April as an in-betweener — the lowest rung of animators, filling in the frames between key drawings[3]. He is dissatisfied from the start: the work feels mechanical, the studio treats animators as interchangeable. Within months he is organising with the union; within two years he is its chief secretary, agitating alongside Isao Takahata, the director who will become his lifelong partner-in-crime.

He is dissatisfied from the start: the work feels mechanical, and the studio treats animators as interchangeable.

Tōei Animation

1964–1973

Miyazaki’s twenties spent inside one studio: key animation, union work, marriage, children — and a first manga drawn under a pseudonym.

1964aged 23

Union chief · key animator

Became chief secretary of Tōei’s animation labour union and shifted from “in-between” to key animation on Fujimaru, and volunteered for The Great Adventure of Horus, Prince of the Sun (released 1968)[3][4].

1965aged 24

Key animator at Tōei · marriage

A key animator on Shōnen Ninja Kaze no Fujimaru (1964–65), beginning work on Hustle Punch (1965–66)[1]. In October he marries fellow Tōei animator Akemi Ōta[2].

1966

Television work

Key animator on Hustle Punch and Rainbow Sentai Robin (1966–67); helped animate early episodes of Sally the Witch (1966–68)[1].

1967First storyboards

Gorō is born

Continued work on Sally the Witch and other TV animation; contributed storyboarding and design to Horus, Prince of the Sun[6]. His son Gorō is born in January[7].

Gorō is born in January — the first of two sons, and a future Ghibli director in his own right.

From the chronology · the Tōei years, 1967

1968aged 27

Horus reaches the screen

The Great Adventure of Horus, Prince of the Sun is released in theatres — Miyazaki among its key animators and storyboard artists. He also works on other Tōei projects leading into 1969[6].

1969aged 28

Flying Phantom Ship · a secret manga · Keisuke

Animates and storyboards Flying Phantom Ship, even proposing scenes involving tanks causing mass hysteria; provides key animation for TV series Moomin and Himitsu no Akko-chan (1969–70)[8]. In April his second son Keisuke is born[7], and he moves his family residence (he later noted moving after births)[5]. From September he writes and illustrates the manga People of the Desert under a pseudonym[10].

1970aged 29

Tokorozawa · Animal Treasure Island

Wraps up People of the Desert (last installments early 1970)[10]; moves to Tokorozawa and continues at Tōei. Begins development on Animal Treasure Island (released 1971), laying out story and key animation[11].

1971aged 30

Resignation · Takahata · Lupin III

In August he resigns from Tōei Animation over creative frustrations[12], joining A-Pro (later Tokyo Movie Shinsha) with Isao Takahata and Yōichi Kotabe — co-directing 17 episodes of Lupin III Part I (1971–72)[12]. With Takahata he also co-develops a planned adaptation of Pippi Longstocking, cancelled after they were unable to secure rights[13].

The trip to Sweden and the pitch came to nothing: Astrid Lindgren declined to grant the rights, and one of Miyazaki’s most cherished early ideas was shelved permanently — the watercolours he made for it survive only as production art. He later recycled Pippi’s spirit in Panda! Go, Panda!’s Mieko-chan, but the series itself never happened.

Astrid Lindgren declined to grant the rights, and one of Miyazaki’s most cherished early ideas was shelved permanently.

From the chronology · the cancelled Pippi Longstocking, 1971

1972aged 31

Panda! Go, Panda! · Sweden

Co-writes and animates the short films Panda! Go, Panda! with Takahata[14]. Designs and animates TV pilots and episodes (The Gutsy Frog, Akado Suzunosuke) and storyboards an abandoned Yuki’s Sun pilot — rediscovered in 2014[15]. Travels to Sweden for the Pippi Longstocking series (cancelled)[16].

1973aged 32

Zuiyō Eizō · Swiss research

Miyazaki and Takahata leave A-Pro and join Zuiyō Eizō (soon Nippon Animation) for World Masterpiece Theater projects[17]. He researches in Switzerland for Heidi, Girl of the Alps[18] — his first foreign trip while handling the Pippi materials[19]. Storyboards for future series go unused.

Nippon Animation · Future Boy Conan

1974–1978

Five formative years of scene design and research travel, ending with Future Boy Conan.

1974aged 33

Heidi, Girl of the Alps

Scene designer and key animator on the broadcast series[20]; contributes to other World Masterpiece Theater series and prepares an Anne of Green Gables segment idea (later directed by Takahata).

1975aged 34

Dog of Flanders · research abroad

Nippon Animation splits from Zuiyō. Brief work on Dog of Flanders; preparatory research in Argentina and Italy for 3000 Leagues in Search of Mother (aired 1976)[21].

1976aged 35

Toward Conan

Completes research trips for 3000 Leagues[21] and works on various Nippon Animation projects; an idea of his is developed into a five-minute pilot for Future Boy Conan.

1977aged 36 · appointed directorFirst direction · TV

Appointed director

At 36, chosen to direct the TV series Future Boy Conan — his first directing job of any kind[22]. Key animation on Nippon’s Rascal the Raccoon and scene design for episodes 1–15 of Takahata’s Anne of Green Gables, before leaving Nippon in 1979[24].

1978aged 37

Future Boy Conan airs

Broadcast on NHK, completing his directorial debut series — 24 of 26 episodes directed[23]. Continued scene design on Rascal the Raccoon and finishes his Anne of Green Gables contributions.

“I had been an animator who could write a little. With Conan, for the first time, I became someone who could see a work through from beginning to end.”

Miyazaki on his directorial debut, reflecting in later interviews

The Castle of Cagliostro

1979–1983

A first theatrical feature — then four years without one, filled instead by a manga that would change everything.

1979aged 38First feature as director

Lupin III: The Castle of Cagliostro

Leaves Nippon Animation for Tokyo Movie Shinsha and directs his first feature film, writing its story[27]. Released December 15, 1979[28]. A commercial disappointment on first release — a failure that kept him away from features for years — though Steven Spielberg reportedly called it one of his favourite adventure films, and its reputation has only grown since.

Feature film · Direction · Story · About 5 months in production

1980aged 39

“Tsutomu Teruki”

At TMS, directs two episodes of Lupin III Part II under the pseudonym “Tsutomu Teruki”[30]; contributes key animation to early episodes of Sherlock Hound.

1981aged 40

Sherlock Hound · Gigantor

Directs six episodes of Sherlock Hound (aired 1984–85)[31] and animates for Gigantor (1980–81), while preparing ideas for next projects.

1982aged 41

Nausicaä begins on paper

Begins writing and illustrating the Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind manga (serial run 1982–94), adapting his own story ideas — initially planned as a film[29]. Continues anime work and develops scripts.

1983aged 42

Drawing on

Continues serializing Nausicaä[29]; plans the animated adaptation and writes screenplays. No feature film is released this year.

1984aged 43

Nausicaä
of the Valley of the Wind

Written and directed by Miyazaki, released March 11, 1984[32]. Produced without an established studio — retroactively credited to Topcraft, because Studio Ghibli does not yet exist. Its success leads him and Isao Takahata to co-found a studio[33].

Feature film · Manga · Direction · About 1 year of film production

Studio Ghibli

1985–1989

Three films in five years, and a brand-new studio finds out what it is.

1985aged 44

Studio Ghibli is founded

Following Nausicaä, Miyazaki co-founds Studio Ghibli with Isao Takahata, Toshio Suzuki, Yasuyoshi Tokuma and others[33]. His final television work follows: episodes 7–12 of Sherlock Hound, completed May 1985[34].

Feature film · Direction · About 2 years from planning to release

1986aged 45

Laputa: Castle in the Sky

Ghibli’s first official feature — written and directed by Miyazaki — is released August 2, 1986[35], a hit in Japan, and proof that Ghibli could pay its bills.

Feature film · Direction · Ghibli’s first official feature · About 1 year of production

A hit in Japan — and proof that Ghibli could pay its bills.

From the chronology · Laputa: Castle in the Sky, 1986

1987aged 46

Storyboarding Totoro

A year of writing and storyboarding My Neighbor Totoro, pitched and put into early production at Ghibli (production spans 1987–88). Planning starts on Kiki’s Delivery Service.

1988aged 47

My Neighbor Totoro

Released April 16, 1988[36]. Written and directed by Miyazaki, inspired by rural Japan and his own childhood — he even voices the Catbus. It was slow to find an audience, but Totoro went on to become the studio’s mascot and its best-loved film.

Feature film · Direction · Voice · Storyboarded over 1 year

1989aged 48

Kiki’s Delivery Service

Released July 29, 1989, produced by Studio Ghibli[36]. Ticket sales and merchandising (from 1990 onward) kept the studio solvent.

Porco Rosso

1990–1996

A pig pilot, a finished manga, a music video — and beneath it all, the long preparation of Princess Mononoke.

1990aged 49

Toward Porco Rosso

Likely begins work on Porco Rosso. Continues writing the ongoing Nausicaä manga and takes a break from feature directing. No film is released in 1990.

1991aged 50

Preparation continues

Continues working on Porco Rosso — storyboards, illustrations, final preparations — while remaining active on the Nausicaä manga and guiding Ghibli projects.

1992aged 51

Porco Rosso

Premiered July 18, 1992 in Japan[37]. Written and directed by Miyazaki — a fantasy adventure of a WWII pilot-turned-pig bounty hunter. Commercially successful (¥2.8 billion[38]) and the first time Disney distributes a Miyazaki film outside Japan.

Feature film · Direction · About 2 years of preparation and production

1993aged 52

Planning Mononoke

The Nausicaä serialization continues; intensive planning for Princess Mononoke begins, with concept art and story development. He oversees Ghibli’s other projects, e.g. Ocean Waves and Pom Poko.

1994aged 53

Nausicaä concludes

The Nausicaä of the Valley of the Wind manga is completed and published (1982–94)[29]. He also storyboards the music-video short “On Your Mark”, which follows in 1995.

The Nausicaä manga is completed — twelve years of serialisation, drawn alongside the films.

From the chronology · 1994

1995aged 54

On Your Mark

Writes and directs the animated music video “On Your Mark”, set to the song by Chage & Aska[39] — a side project during a creative lull on Princess Mononoke.

1996aged 55

The year of Mononoke

Devoted to producing Princess Mononoke: extensive storyboarding and animation planning. Its environmental themes prove controversial, but the film is completed by late 1996.

1997aged 56

Princess
Mononoke

Released in July 1997 — the first animated film to win Japan’s Academy Picture of the Year, and for a while the highest-grossing film in Japanese history[40]. Ghibli’s name travelled abroad with it.

Feature film · Direction · Japan Academy Picture of the Year · About 4 years of planning and production

Spirited Away

1998–2004

A gap year, a spirit world, and the awards that carry Miyazaki around the globe.

1998aged 57

A gap year

After Mononoke: a year of conceiving new ideas. He had begun writing the screenplay for Spirited Away[41].

Feature film · Direction · About 3 years from story work to premiere

1999aged 58

Refining the story

Refines the story for Spirited Away; production officially begins in 2000[41].

2000aged 59

Production begins

Spirited Away enters production[41] — written, directed and animated by Miyazaki about a young girl in a spirit world. He works closely with producer Toshio Suzuki and meets Disney’s John Lasseter for international support.

I made this film for ten-year-old girls. That’s it — the daughters of my friends.

Miyazaki, on why he made Spirited Away, in interviews around its release

2001aged 60

Spirited Away

Released July 20, 2001[42]. Japan’s highest-grossing film ever (¥31.68 billion); wins the Golden Bear at Berlin in 2002 and the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature in 2003[43].

Feature film · Direction · Golden Bear · Academy Award · About 3 years from concept to screen

2002aged 61

The Golden Bear

Wins the Golden Bear at the Berlin Film Festival for Spirited Away (February). Story work continues on Howl’s Moving Castle, an adaptation of Diana Wynne Jones’s novel; he oversees Spirited Away’s international releases.

2003aged 62

The Oscar

Spirited Away wins the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature (March). Miyazaki does not attend the ceremony — he was in Japan, and was reported to be troubled by the war in Iraq, which had begun days earlier[43]. Work continues on Howl’s Moving Castle, completing its animation; he voices a minor character in Spirited Away’s English dub.

He does not attend the Oscar ceremony — he was in Japan, reportedly troubled by the war in Iraq, which had begun days earlier.

From the chronology · the Oscar, March 2003

2004aged 63

Howl’s Moving Castle

Premieres at the Locarno Film Festival (September); released in Japan November 20[44]. Written and directed by Miyazaki, a critical success — though he later expressed disappointment at its box office relative to expectations. An Academy Award nomination follows in 2005.

Ponyo · The Wind Rises

2005–2012

Two more features, a growing public voice on peace and nuclear power, and a national honour.

2005aged 64

Nomination · Ponyo begins

Oscar-nominated for Howl’s Moving Castle; at home it is honoured as Animation of the Year by the Japanese Academy. Early development begins on Ponyo on the Cliff by the Sea. Increasingly vocal in pacifist interviews abroad about Japan’s postwar peace constitution.

Increasingly vocal in pacifist interviews abroad about Japan’s postwar peace constitution.

From the chronology · 2005

2006aged 65

Locked in the studio

Production on Ponyo begins[45]. By March he is sketching storyboards and painting art as preparation. He gives interviews critical of government policies, championing pacifism and environmentalism.

2007

Pastels for a tsunami

The first shot of Ponyo is completed by March[46]; he uses new techniques — drawing with pastels — for the tsunami sequence. At 66, he speaks publicly about his age and remaining years[47].

It’s good to be alive.

The message Miyazaki said he most wanted to hand children with Ponyo

2008aged 67

Ponyo on the Cliff by the Sea

Released July 19, 2008[48]. Written and directed by Miyazaki, a family-friendly aquatic fantasy that earned ¥13.6 billion at the Japanese box office.

Feature film · Direction · Over 2 years in production

2009aged 68

Kaze Tachinu

Publishes the manga The Wind Has Risen (Kaze Tachinu), a semi-autobiographical story about aviation engineer Jirō Horikoshi[49] — preparing the way for a feature film. No new film this year, but continued advising of Studio Ghibli.

2010aged 69

Toward the last feature

Finalizing the film The Wind Rises: screenplay written, animation supervised. He voices support for young talent at Ghibli and comments on art education.

2011aged 70

A banner against nuclear power

After the March Tōhoku earthquake, tsunami and Fukushima disaster, he turns openly political: in June, at his direction, a banner hung on Studio Ghibli’s building reads —

Work on The Wind Rises continues.

“We want to make movies with electricity that’s not from nuclear power plants.”[50]

Banner hung on Studio Ghibli’s building at Miyazaki’s direction, June 2011

2012aged 71

Person of Cultural Merit

In November the Japanese government designates him a Person of Cultural Merit, recognizing outstanding contributions to culture[51]. Finishing touches on The Wind Rises as Ghibli prepares its release; mostly quiet on political issues, focused on the film.

The Wind Rises · Retirement

2013–2015

One last feature, a farewell announcement, an honorary Oscar — and protest.

2013aged 72

The Wind Rises

Released July 20, 2013[52] — the biographical drama of Jirō Horikoshi, and the highest-grossing Japanese film of the year, earning critical acclaim and a Japan Academy Award. After its release, Miyazaki announces his retirement from feature animation[53].

Feature film · Direction · Retirement announcement · About 4 years from manga to screen

At the Tokyo press conference in September he told reporters: “I know I have said I would retire many times before. This time I am quite serious.”[61] He was widely reported to be exhausted by the film’s production and by anxiety over its reception.

2014aged 73

The honorary Oscar

Awarded an Academy Honorary Award (November) for lifetime impact on animation[54]. In September he gives a rare interview in Ghibli’s Neppu magazine criticizing proposed changes to Japan’s pacifist constitution[55]. The Wind Rises releases internationally; he begins to relax after retirement.

It was not an untroubled farewell: in Japan the film was attacked from both left and right — for romanticising the designer of the Zero fighter, and for being insufficiently patriotic. Miyazaki called the Zero “a beautiful dream, cursed” and accepted that the film would displease almost everyone.

2015aged 74

Protest

In July he joins protests against changes to Japan’s security laws and constitution, telling reporters Japan should never use military force to counter China[56], and supports a fund opposing a new U.S. military base in Okinawa[57]. Creatively disillusioned; no new projects announced.

The Boy and the Heron

2016–2022

Retirement lasts exactly as long as it needs to. A new film is announced — and takes seven years.

2016aged 75

The return

Announces his return from retirement to begin a new feature — initially titled How Do You Live?, later The Boy and the Heron[53]. Storyboarding and research begin at Studio Ghibli; the film will take several years.

Retirement lasts exactly as long as it needs to.

From the chronology · the return, 2016

2017aged 76

Quiet at Koganei

Works quietly on The Boy and the Heron: storyboards and concept art at Ghibli’s Koganei studios. Still holds his title as honorary chairman of Studio Ghibli.

2018aged 77

Scene by scene

The long production continues. At 77 he storyboards the film scene by scene, as with Ponyo and The Wind Rises; reports note a fluid script and evolving production. No release announced.

2019aged 78

“10 Years with Hayao Miyazaki”

The NHK documentary series airs (February–March), showing his work on Ponyo and Wind Rises and hinting at his thoughts on the new film. He remains in production, commenting on aging and persisting despite claims he might retire for good.

He remains in production, commenting on aging and persisting despite claims he might retire for good.

From the chronology · “10 Years with Hayao Miyazaki”, 2019

2020aged 79

Finalizing in silence

Finalizes The Boy and the Heron (working title How Do You Live?). The pandemic has little public effect on progress — the film was already moving slowly. No interviews; focus on animation.

2021aged 80

Half complete

Studio Ghibli announces the film is 50% complete by mid-2021. Deep in storyboarding and colour layouts, healthy and active — though work is slower than in earlier decades.

Half complete by mid-2021 — deep in storyboards, healthy and active, though slower than in earlier decades.

From the chronology · 2021

2022

Wrapped at 81

Completes production of The Boy and the Heron; Ghibli begins theatrical release plans for 2023. At 81 he advises Ghibli projects with no other film plans, living privately but occasionally visiting the studio in Koganei.

2023aged 82

The Boy
and the Heron

Premiered in Japan on July 14, 2023[58] — a fantasy-adventure loosely based on Genzaburō Yoshino’s novel How Do You Live?, written and directed by Miyazaki at 82. The critics went along; audiences showed up without being asked.

Feature film · Direction · Written at 82 · 7 years in development

He has said the film was made with his grandson in mind — a grandfather’s answer to the question of what to leave behind. He drew with a weakening hand, aware it might be his last film, and told producers there would be no trailer and no marketing: “Because I don’t know what it means myself.” The studio released it anyway, in silence.

I made this film for my grandson. It’s my way of saying goodbye.

Miyazaki on The Boy and the Heron, as reported around its premiere

2024aged 83

A second Oscar

The Boy and the Heron wins the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature at the 96th Oscars[59] — his second competitive Oscar, after Spirited Away. He is also awarded the Ramon Magsaysay Award, often called the “Asian Nobel.” Studio Ghibli vice-president Junichi Nishioka notes he is already “coming into the office with new ideas”[60] — no firm plans to retire again.

2025aged 84

Still coming into the office

Having turned 84 in January, he remains active at Studio Ghibli, speaking of exploring new story ideas[60]. No new film confirmed, but he continues to sketch and plan, supporting the next generation at Ghibli — including son Gorō’s films.

19652025

Sixty years after joining Tōei Animation, he is still coming into the office with new ideas.
